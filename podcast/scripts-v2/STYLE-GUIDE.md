# Walk Steadfast · Script Style Guide (v2)

This guide is for writing every Walk Steadfast episode from E007 on. It turns research on strong co-hosted podcasts and radio shows into rules we can check on the page before a script is recorded. Sources are listed at the end, and each rule notes where it comes from.

---

## 1. The one-sentence version

Two men at a table work through one hard part of following Jesus. Wes drives, Jonathon colors. The first thirty seconds earn the next twenty minutes. Every story ends in a point, every segment ends on its best line, and the listener is always the third person at the table.

---

## 2. Roles: driver and color

On radio, the play-by-play voice calls the action and the color commentator adds the analysis, the story and the opinion in the gaps. If the listener can't tell who is calling the game, the broadcast fails. On a talk show the same split works when both players are strong. Radio coaches call a show where one host takes about 80% of the mic and the other just reacts a "big planet with a small moon," and those shows rate worse than real ensembles. (Commentator series; Radio Ink, Lane 2021 and McHugh 2022)

### Wes Hart, the driver

- **Owns the frame.** He delivers the hook, names the question of the day, introduces each segment and calls the turns ("Let's take the next few minutes on the why").
- **Keeps time out loud.** He drops a time check two or three times an episode ("We're about halfway," "We've got about six minutes"). It tells the listener the show has a shape and an end. (PTI's on-screen rundown and clock do this job on TV.)
- **Asks the main questions.** The big "why" and "what now" questions are his.
- **Tells the anchor story.** In most episodes he carries the central anecdote, then hands off.
- **Makes room.** He pauses, lets Jonathon finish, and gives him a handoff by name when it's his turn. A host who leaves no space gets a sidekick, not a co-host. (Radio Ink, McHugh 2022)

### Jonathon, the color commentator

- **Brings his own material.** Every episode he needs at least two of his own stories or arguments from his own life (sales, Dani, Ruby and Theo, his mom, his dad, coming to faith late), prepped separately from Wes's. Separate prep keeps surprise in the room. (Podcast Performance Coach #254)
- **Pushes back for real.** At least one segment per episode where he argues the other side, at full strength, and is allowed to stay partly unconvinced. PTI's whole engine is "yell at each other, love each other." (Washington City Paper, Kornheiser quote)
- **Plays the listener.** He asks the question a smart listener would ask, the way Monica Padman grounds Armchair Expert. He's the audience's stand-in, not Wes's echo. (Armchair Expert analyses)
- **Is funny about himself first.** Car Talk's warmth came from two experts who laughed at themselves before they laughed at anyone else. Jonathon's humor should usually be at his own expense or the situation's, never at the listener's. (Jacobs Media, Berman interview; Vulture)
- **"Yes, and," not "wow."** One-word reactions ("Oof." "Wow." "Hmm.") block the conversation. Build on the line instead. (Radio Ink, McHugh 2022)

### Mic-share and short-line rules (checked on every draft)

| Measure | Target |
|---|---|
| Jonathon's share of spoken words | 35-50% |
| Jonathon lines under 12 words (one-liners and quick add-on questions) | under 20% of his lines |
| Wes lines under 12 words | no cap. The driver's short prompts and time checks keep things moving. |
| Longest uninterrupted Wes turn | about 120 words, then a handoff or a reaction |

A Jonathon line counts as short when it has fewer than 12 spoken words. Count them before a table read.

### Jonathon's voice

He's a late-30s guy from the Houston suburbs who manages a sales team. He talks like a modern regular guy: quick, plain and a little dry. Phones, group texts, calendars, kids' activities and work reviews are his world.

- **Use:** "Honestly." "Okay, hold on." "Here's my problem with that." "That's wild." "I'll be real with you."
- **Avoid:** country or backwoods phrasing (no "y'all," "reckon," "fixin' to," "yonder," "feed store," "Lord willin'"), preacher cadence, and anything that leans on a deep-bass "radio voice." He shouldn't sound older than he is.
- **Good:** "At work I've got a reminder for my reminders. At home I've got zero follow-up."
- **Bad:** "Well, I reckon a man's gotta tend his own fences."

## 3. The open: hook in thirty seconds

**What the research says.** PodAnalyst studied retention on 100,000+ episodes and found that a listener who makes it past the first 30 seconds has about a 94% chance of still being there at the quarter mark. The first 30 seconds is where you lose people. Only 7 of the top 20 podcasts in one survey ran intros longer than 30 seconds, and 6 had little more than a jingle and "welcome to the show." Pre-roll ads and housekeeping before the first real sentence cause steep early drops. A clip lifted from later in the episode can backfire, because the listener feels déjà vu or thinks they missed something. (PodAnalyst / Lejnine; The Podcast Host)

**Rules**

1. **The first words are the episode.** Open on the most intense true line, or name the problem plainly. No music before the first word. A sting of 2 seconds or less is allowed after the hook.
2. **Write a fresh hook. Don't preview a clip.** The open is its own short scene, written for that slot.
3. **The show ID comes after the hook and stays under 15 seconds.** Name, name, and the question of the day.
4. **Promise a payoff.** End the open with a specific forward tease the listener can only collect by staying ("Before we're done, I'll tell you the day and the hour I'm making mine").
5. **No housekeeping in the open.** Plugs, subscribe asks and schedules go to the close or into the show notes.
6. **The open is scripted word for word.** So is the close. The middle is fully written for this pilot, but a table read should loosen it.

**Template**

```
WES: [The most intense true line, or the problem in one breath.]
JONATHON: [Names it, sharpens it, or turns it, in his own words.]
WES: [The stakes. Who it costs.]
[STING 2s]
WES: This is Walk Steadfast. I'm Wes Hart.
JONATHON: And I'm Jonathon.
WES: [Today's one question, in plain words.] [Who it's for: "If you..."] [A specific forward tease.]
```

## 4. Story arc: hook, tension, resolution

Ira Glass's rule for This American Life: an **anecdote** (this happened, then this, then this) creates momentum because you can't stop wanting to know what happens next. It has to be followed by a **moment of reflection** that says what it means. "You can't just have an anecdote. It's got to mean something." The show alternates them: anecdote, reflection, anecdote, reflection. (Current, "Mo' Better Radio"; Transom)

**Episode shape (about 20 minutes, about 3,000 words)**

| Movement | Share | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | under 5% | The problem in its sharpest form, plus the promise |
| Tension | about 60% | The anchor story, then why men do this, then who pays, then Scripture raising the bar, then honest pushback. Each step should make the problem harder to dodge, not easier. |
| Turn | about 10% | The emotional peak. Usually a story or a question the listener has to answer for himself. |
| Resolution | about 25% | What the hosts are actually doing, the listener's challenge, and the close |

**Rules**

- **Every story gets its reflection, and fast.** The meaning comes within a line or two of the story ending, and it's usually the other host who says it.
- **Concrete beats abstract.** "Passed the potatoes, talked about the Cowboys" beats "things were polite." Specific detail is how a listener sees a room he can't see.
- **One step off topic, then back.** A tangent is fine. Two in a row loses a listener who just tuned in. (Tommy Kramer)
- **Leave on the high note.** End a segment on its best line, then turn. "In storytelling, the moment you feel like you could end, you probably should." (Radio Update, "A Path to a Payoff")
- **Honest loose ends are allowed.** A disagreement can stay open, and so can a fight that isn't finished. The episode's main question still has to be answered.

## 5. Pacing and segments

- **Rate.** Radio hosts and podcasters run about 150-160 words per minute. We estimate at **150 wpm** (3,000 words is about 20 minutes). (ImprovePodcast, citing the National Center for Voice and Speech)
- **Segment length.** Most segments run 2-4 minutes, the way PTI gives each topic a few minutes and moves on before it's exhausted. One longer story segment per episode is fine. (PTI, Wikipedia)
- **Vary the speed.** Go fast in banter and slow on the turn. The longest pause in the episode (about 2 seconds) belongs to the most important line.
- **Turn length.** Most turns run 1-5 sentences. A story turn can run longer, but break it with a real reaction every 100-120 words.
- **Use names at handoffs.** Two male voices can blur, so say "Jonathon" or "Wes" at turns, especially in the first five minutes. (Podcast Performance Coach #254)

## 6. Banter, handoffs and teases

**Banter**

- Banter comes out of the topic. Don't paste it onto the topic. The best laugh in an episode about waiting should be about waiting ("There's no traffic in the Hill Country").
- Callbacks pay off. Something set up early (the cease-fire, "we'll see," the reminder at 7:01) comes back at the close.
- Disagreement stays warm. Push hard on the idea, never on the man.

**Handoffs** (make the conversation change hands on purpose)

- By name: "Jonathon, you've been sitting on something. Push."
- By invitation: "I've got a third reason, but I want yours first."
- By reflection: one host lands the story, and the other says what it means.
- Team tease: one host sets the tease and the other fills in a detail. (Radio Ink, Lane, "7 Tips for Magnetic Teasers")

**Teases**

- Tease one thing, specifically, and make it a question the listener wants answered. "Mr. Abernathy's next" is a label. "A man asked me the same four words for six years" is a tease. (Radio Update, "The Enticing Tease")
- A tease points to the hook of what's coming, not to its ending. (Andy Meadows)
- Use one horizontal tease per episode, pointing to the next show ("Friday I'll tell you whether I made the call").

## 7. The listener is the third chair

Radio is intimate. One voice, one person, usually alone in a car or a kitchen. Coaches tell talent to stay **singular**: "you," never "all of you" or "everybody out there." A test match commentator put it bluntly: banter that turns into in-jokes is "exclusionary and alienating." SmartLess is a good model. Sean Hayes stops to explain any inside reference for his sister in Wisconsin, and listeners love the show for it. (Tommy Kramer; Ask MetaFilter, public-radio training; The Cricket Monthly, TMS; Variety on SmartLess)

**Rules**

- Talk to "you," singular. "If you've got a call you keep not making..."
- Stop at least once per segment to put the listener in the scene ("Look around your house this week...").
- A story a listener hasn't heard gets one line of setup, every time, even if it's been told before.

**Banned (inside baseball)**

- "Like we were saying before we started." "You told me off-air." "As you know." "You know the story." "I've heard you tell this fifty times."
- "Back in episode four..." (Describe the idea, not the episode number.)
- References to wardrobe, set, crew, production or how the show is made.
- Nicknames or running jokes that haven't been explained on air.

## 8. Scripture

- **Verify every reference against the translation we print (NIV)** before the table read, word for word, including punctuation, and log the date in the script header.
- **Quote short and read it aloud.** One or two verses, read by one host. Wes often hands the reading to Jonathon.
- **Context in two sentences.** Say who's writing, to whom, and what came just before. No proof-texting.
- **Let Scripture raise the tension, not just settle it.** The verse should make the problem harder to dodge before the hosts work out how to obey it.
- **Grace in the same breath as conviction.** Naming sin is for confession and forgiveness, not shame.
- **A paraphrase is not a quote.** Anything inside quotation marks must be exact NIV.

---

## 9. [BOARD] cues

Board cues are on-screen text for the video cut: Scripture or a key idea.

- **3-5 per episode.**
- **Never repeat the spoken words.** The board adds a second layer, such as a related verse, the verse's context, or the idea restated in different words. If the hosts read James 4:17 aloud, the board shows James 4:14, not 4:17. Check: no run of four or more words on the board may appear in the spoken script.
- **Short enough to read in about 5 seconds.** One idea per board. Scripture boards carry the reference and "(NIV)."
- **The audio has to stand on its own.** No line may depend on the board ("as you can see...").

---

## 10. The close: word for word, ends on the CTA

**What the research says.** Strong outros run about **20-40 seconds** and do three things in order: wrap the value in one line, give one primary next step, and say exactly where to take it. Stacked asks get ignored. Don't introduce a new idea in the outro. (PodcastingProTips; Lupa Digital; The Audacity to Podcast)

**Rules**

1. **Carry this.** One line from each host. Wes states the episode's idea. Jonathon states it his way, often as a callback.
2. **A horizontal tease.** One sentence pointing to the next episode.
3. **One action, one place.** The challenge plus "tell us," pointing to **walksteadfast.com** and **@steadfastwalk**.
4. **The last words are the CTA.** Nothing after it but the sting.

**Template**

```
WES: Here's what I want you to carry out of here. [The idea in one or two sentences.]
JONATHON: [The same idea his way. A callback if there is one.]
WES: [Horizontal tease.] [The challenge in five words.]
JONATHON: [The action.] Then tell us at walksteadfast.com, and find us at @steadfastwalk.
[STING]
```

---

## 11. Script format

- Speaker labels in caps with a colon: `WES:` / `JONATHON:`.
- Light stage cues in square brackets: `[beat]`, `[laughs]`, `[both laugh]`, `[pause 2s]`, `[taps the table twice]`. No more than about one cue per five lines.
- Segment headers are for producers and are never spoken.
- `[STING 2s]` is the only music cue inside the episode.
- The header lists the runtime target, word count, Scripture (verified, with date) and board cues.

---

## 12. Pre-flight checklist

- [ ] First spoken line is the hook. No music or housekeeping first.
- [ ] The open ends with a specific forward tease, and the episode pays it off.
- [ ] Spoken words land between 2,800 and 3,200.
- [ ] Wes introduces every segment and gives at least two time checks.
- [ ] Jonathon has at least two of his own stories or arguments and one real pushback.
- [ ] Jonathon lines under 12 words make up less than 20% of his lines.
- [ ] There's an arc: hook, then tension that builds, then a turn, then resolution.
- [ ] Every quotation of Scripture is exact NIV and verified, with the date logged.
- [ ] There are 3-5 board cues, and none repeats four or more spoken words in a row.
- [ ] No off-air references, episode-number callbacks or unexplained in-jokes.
- [ ] Nothing in the script refers to how the show is produced.
- [ ] The close is word for word and the last spoken words are the CTA (walksteadfast.com, @steadfastwalk).
