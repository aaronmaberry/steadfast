**Arc.** *Hook:* Wes hasn't called his brother in two months, and it feels like peace. *Tension:* it's a cease-fire, and somebody else is paying for it. Men wait from fear, comfort and the need to feel ready. James and Jesus raise the bar. Jonathon pushes back on guilt, on "wait for the Lord," and on his own father. Wes's dad waited 43 years to say four words. *Turn:* Mr. Abernathy's question, asked to the listener. *Resolution:* Wes's call goes on the calendar, Jonathon answers "we'll see" and schedules the review, then the challenge and the close.

## OPEN (word for word)

WES: I haven't talked to my older brother since Thanksgiving. Two months. And I'll be honest with you, it's been the most peaceful two months I've had all year.

JONATHON: [beat] Wes, that's not peace. That's a cease-fire. Everybody's dug in, nobody's shooting, and nobody's going home either.

WES: [beat] Yeah. And somebody's paying for it. It just isn't me.

[STING 2s]

WES: This is Walk Steadfast. I'm Wes Hart.

JONATHON: And I'm Jonathon.

WES: Today, one question. Why does a man wait on the thing he already knows he should do, and why does the waiting feel so much like peace? If there's a call you keep not making, or a conversation you keep pushing to next week, pull up a chair. And stay with us, because before we're done, I'm going to tell you the day and the hour I'm making mine.

JONATHON: And I'm holding him to it. I'm a sales guy. I set reminders for everything, and I'm about to set one for him.

## SEGMENT 1 · The quiet

WES: So here's how I got here. I've got two brothers. My little brother's forty-seven, lives up in Fort Worth, funniest man I know. My older brother's fifty-eight. He stayed on our dad's place after Dad passed, and he's the one who takes care of our mom. She's seventy-eight, bad hip, still lives by herself.

JONATHON: And I'm guessing "still lives by herself" is where the trouble starts. Because I've watched that exact conversation blow up two Thanksgivings in a row in my wife's family. Somebody thinks Mom needs help, somebody thinks Mom's fine, and the one who lives closest is just tired.

WES: That's exactly where it went. We're at Mom's, it comes up at the table, it gets loud. And at some point my older brother looks right at me, in front of everybody, and says, "You've always let me handle Mom."

JONATHON: Oh, man. In front of everybody. What'd you say back?

WES: Nothing. That's what I want you to hear. I didn't argue. I didn't agree. I finished my plate, and I told everybody I had to leave early to beat the traffic.

JONATHON: [laughs] Wes. Your mom lives in the Hill Country. There's no traffic in the Hill Country. There's one stoplight and a Dairy Queen.

WES: [laughs] You know that. I know that. My brother definitely knew that. And since then, nothing. Christmas was polite. We passed the potatoes, talked about the Cowboys, hugged at the door. And on the drive home I told my wife, "See? It's settled down." And I meant it.

JONATHON: Okay, that one lands, because I've got the same thing going on in my own kitchen. My wife, Dani, and I had a fight back in October about my mom and the holidays, who gets which day. A real fight. And then it just stopped. The kids needed a bath, we were wiped out, and we never came back to it. For three months I've been calling that "we moved on." We didn't move on. We just stopped talking.

WES: So you've got a cease-fire too.

JONATHON: I've got a cease-fire, and here's what I figured out about cease-fires. They don't end the war. They just schedule it for later. Ours is scheduled for Easter, because Easter's the next holiday with my mom. I can feel it coming like weather.

[BOARD: Jeremiah 6:14 (NIV) · "'Peace, peace,' they say, when there is no peace."]

WES: If you've got one of these, hear this. Quiet isn't the same as settled. Settled means somebody said the hard thing and it got dealt with. Quiet just means everybody agreed not to bring it up.

## SEGMENT 2 · Who's paying

WES: Let's take the next few minutes on the why. Not just me. Men. Why do we sit on stuff we already know? Jonathon, you first.

JONATHON: Mine runs my whole life, honestly. We're scared of making it worse. Right now there's no fight. If I bring it back up, maybe there's a fight. So doing nothing feels like the safe play. It even feels mature, like I'm protecting the peace, when really I'm just protecting me.

WES: That's the big one. The second one's comfort. Doing the thing costs you something today. Waiting costs something too, but the bill comes later, and it's spread out thin.

JONATHON: And here's the part that should bother us. Half the time that bill doesn't even come to the guy who's waiting. Somebody else opens the envelope.

WES: [beat] Yeah. Since August, my wife has been driving my mom to physical therapy every Tuesday. My brother handles the doctors, the pharmacy, the guy who fixes her porch. And I left early to beat the traffic. That's why it felt like peace. I'm not the one paying.

JONATHON: That's the line, and it's brutal. When you wait, somebody else is usually covering for you. And I already know who's covering for me. She's the one doing bedtime alone on the nights I'm "just catching up on email."

[BOARD: Delay isn't free. Check whose name is on the bill.]

WES: So look around your house this week. Whatever you've been waiting on, find out who's covering for it. It's usually somebody you love.

JONATHON: Can I give you the work version? It's the same thing wearing a tie. In sales there's a saying: the deal doesn't die in the meeting, it dies in the follow-up. Great meeting, everybody shakes hands, and then nobody calls anybody for three weeks. By the time you circle back, they signed with somebody else. Nobody ever said no. It just died of waiting.

WES: Died of waiting.

JONATHON: And here's what gets me. At work I'm a follow-up machine. I've got reminders for my reminders. At home I've got zero follow-up. I run a whole team on "close the loop," and I've had an open loop with my wife since October.

WES: There's a third reason, and it's the sneakiest. We wait until we feel ready. And the feeling never comes first. I'm fifty-two, and I have never once felt ready to make a hard phone call. You make the call, and the ready shows up halfway through. Or after. Or never, and you made it anyway.

JONATHON: I did that with God for about five years. In my twenties I'd go to church with my mom on Easter, and there'd be this moment where I'd think, okay, this is real, I should do something about this. And right behind it: not yet. When I settle down. When I stop going out every weekend. Like I had to clean myself up first. Like brushing your teeth before the dentist.

WES: [laughs] Getting ready for church.

JONATHON: Getting ready for church. I even picked a date. When our first kid's born, I'll get serious. And then Ruby was on the way, and it hit me that I was about to bring a baby home to a guy who was still getting ready. And the getting ready was never going to be done. That's the part nobody told me. You don't clean up and then come to Jesus. You come, and he does the cleaning.

WES: Same order for me. Different year, same order.

## SEGMENT 3 · The Word

WES: We've got a little under fifteen minutes, and I want to get the Bible open, because Scripture has a word for this, and it's sharper than any of ours. Jonathon, you've got younger eyes. James chapter four, verse seventeen.

JONATHON: "If anyone, then, knows the good they ought to do and doesn't do it, it is sin for them."

[BOARD: James 4:14 (NIV) · "Why, you do not even know what will happen tomorrow. What is your life? You are a mist that appears for a little while and then vanishes."]

WES: Quick context. Right before that, James is talking to people making big plans. This city, this business, this money. People who assume tomorrow is a sure thing. And then he lands it. If you already know the good and you're sitting on it, God doesn't call that being careful. He calls it sin.

JONATHON: Okay, I'll be real, this is where I get uncomfortable. "Sin" is a heavy word for not calling your brother. And guilt has never once gotten me off the couch. Guilt just makes me feel bad on the couch. So if we tell a guy "you're sinning," aren't we just piling on?

WES: Fair question. I think the strong word is on purpose. We've got a whole vocabulary that makes waiting sound wise. "I'm processing." "I'm giving it space." "I'm being patient." James cuts right through it. You knew the good. You didn't do it. That has a name. But he names it so you can bring it to God, be forgiven, and go do the thing. Not so you'll sit there feeling terrible.

JONATHON: Right, because feeling bad about waiting is just more waiting. It's waiting with extra steps.

WES: [laughs] That's exactly it. And Jesus is even more direct. Matthew five. He says if you're at the altar, about to offer your gift, and you remember your brother has something against you, leave the gift right there. "First go and be reconciled to them; then come and offer your gift."

JONATHON: Hold on. So the making-it-right comes before the worship? That flips my whole instinct. My instinct is, I'll pray about it, get my heart right, and then someday I'll deal with the person. And Jesus is basically saying, no, set the offering down and go knock on the door first.

WES: Set it down and go. And look at who has the grudge in that verse. It's not "if you're mad at your brother." It's "your brother or sister has something against you." That's me at Thanksgiving. He's the one holding something. And Jesus says the next move is mine.

## SEGMENT 4 · Pushback

WES: Jonathon, you've been sitting on something this whole time. Go ahead and push.

JONATHON: Yeah, here's my problem. The Bible tells you to wait all the time. "Wait for the Lord." It's all over the Psalms. So which is it? Is waiting faith, or is waiting sin? Because if I'm a guy listening to this on my commute, I'm confused.

WES: That's a real one. Psalm twenty-seven ends, "Wait for the Lord; be strong and take heart and wait for the Lord." That's waiting as faith. But look at what you're waiting on. Waiting on the Lord means waiting on God to do what only God can do. Heal somebody. Open a door. Change a heart. The other kind is waiting on yourself. God already showed you the good, and you're waiting until you feel like doing it. That's not faith. That's me in a recliner, praying the recliner will get up for me.

JONATHON: [laughs] Okay, so the test is, am I waiting on God, or am I waiting on me? And I'll be honest, when I run that test, it's me almost every time. God's not the one who's stuck. He didn't leave Thanksgiving early.

WES: [laughs] No, he did not.

JONATHON: Second problem, and this one's harder. Sometimes waiting is just wisdom. Not every hard conversation should happen tonight. If I bring up my mom with Dani at ten o'clock on a Tuesday after our four-year-old's been up twice, that's not obedience. That's bad timing. Sometimes waiting is just not being an idiot.

WES: Agreed. So here's my test. Patience picks a time. Waiting doesn't. If I say, I'll talk to my mom first so I know where she stands, then call my brother Wednesday, that's patience. If I say I'll call him when it feels right, that's waiting. "When it feels right" never shows up on a calendar.

[BOARD: Patience has a reason and a date. Waiting has a mood and a "someday."]

JONATHON: Okay, one more push, because I think there's a third kind you're skipping. My dad wasn't around when I was a kid. A while back he sent me a friend request. I haven't accepted it, and I haven't deleted it. It's just sitting there. And part of me says that's not laziness, that's protecting my family. I don't know who he is now. So is that patience, or am I hiding?

WES: [beat] I'm not going to give you a cheap answer on that. My brother's never hurt me. I'm just scared of an awkward phone call. Your dad is a different weight. You don't have to decide tonight whether to accept it. But put a date on deciding. Not on accepting. On deciding. Because "pending" forever isn't a decision. It's waiting with good reasons.

JONATHON: [beat] I don't totally buy that yet. I think part of me hides behind "it's complicated," because that's easier than saying I'm scared. But I'll give you this. I'll pick a date to decide. I'm just not saying it out loud yet.

WES: That's honest. And if you've got a "pending" like that, same move. You don't need the answer yet. You need the day you'll decide.

## SEGMENT 5 · The question

WES: We've got about seven minutes, and I want to use them on two things. The longest wait I ever watched, and a question a man asked me for six years. [beat] My dad never said "I'm proud of you." Not until he was in a hospital bed on oxygen, and I was forty-three, holding a cup of ice chips. I always told that like it was a sad thing about him. This week I saw it as waiting. I think he was proud of me at my first Little League game. When I got married. When my daughter was born. And every time, he waited. For the right moment. For it to not be awkward between two men who didn't say things.

JONATHON: [quietly] And the right moment ended up being a hospital room. That one gets me, because I've got a seven-year-old and a four-year-old, and I don't want them hearing it for the first time over a heart monitor.

WES: I'm grateful he said it. I've thanked God a hundred times that he said it. But I'd have liked to hear it at sixteen. Now I say it to my kids so much my daughter says I sound like a greeting card.

JONATHON: Honestly, be the greeting card. A greeting card beats a hospital room every time. And I think that's the real list, isn't it? It's not the gutters and the garage. It's words. "I'm proud of you." "I'm sorry." "I was wrong." Every guy I know is carrying at least one of those around, waiting on a better moment that's never coming.

WES: Which brings me to Mr. Abernathy. When I was in my thirties, an older man at our church took me to breakfast every other Thursday for six years. Same diner, same booth. And somewhere in the middle of every breakfast, he'd set his fork down and tap the table twice, like this.

[Wes taps the table twice.]

WES: And he'd ask the same four words. "What are you waiting on?"

JONATHON: The same four words, every other Thursday, for six years? What did you even say?

WES: The first year, I'd say, "For work to slow down." And he'd say, "How long have you been waiting on that?" I'd do the math. Four years. Five. And he'd just pick his fork back up. He never lectured me, not one time. He let me hear my own answer. And by the third year, I'd have the thing done before Thursday, because I knew he was going to ask.

JONATHON: That's better than every accountability framework I've ever sat through at work. No app, no spreadsheet, no quarterly goals. Just a guy who is definitely going to ask you.

WES: So I want to do it for him, one time, for you. Not to preach. Just the way he did it.

[Wes taps the table twice.]

WES: What are you waiting on?

[pause 2s]

## SEGMENT 6 · What we're actually doing

WES: All right. I said I'd tell you mine. Saturday, I called my little brother. That was the easy one. Forty minutes, and he told me a story about his neighbor's goat that I'm not allowed to repeat on a Christian show.

JONATHON: You have to give us one detail. Just one. Is the goat okay?

WES: [laughs] The goat is fine. The neighbor is not. That's all you get. And then Sunday night I sat down to call my older brother, and I looked at his name for a long time. Long enough that my wife walked by twice. And I didn't call.

JONATHON: Okay, so what's the plan? Because "I looked at his name for a while" isn't a plan.

WES: Here's the plan. It's on the calendar. Wednesday, seven p.m., after he's in from feeding. I'm going to tell him he was mostly right. Not all the way. Mostly. And I'm saying it on the show so I can't back out.

JONATHON: And I've got my reminder set for seven-oh-one. I will be texting you. Now me, and I've got two. The first one's small. My daughter, Ruby, is seven, and she's been asking for a dog for over a year. Posters. Drawings taped to my mirror. She made a slide deck, Wes. And for a year I've said, "We'll see." And she knows exactly what "we'll see" means.

WES: It means no.

JONATHON: It means no, and I don't have the guts to say no. That's waiting. That's me not giving an answer so I don't have to watch her be sad. So Dani and I sat down and actually decided. After her little brother, Theo, turns five, in August. We circled it on the kitchen calendar. Ruby cried for about thirty seconds and then asked if she could name it now.

[BOARD: Proverbs 3:28 (NIV) · "Do not say to your neighbor, 'Come back tomorrow and I'll give it to you'—when you already have it with you."]

WES: And?

JONATHON: Pancake. We don't know what kind of dog it is. Could be a lab, could be a poodle. It's Pancake. The second one's work. I've got a guy on my team who's been underperforming for six months, and I've moved his review three times. I keep telling myself it's to give him a fair shot. It's not. It's because I want him to like me. So it's Thursday, and I'm going to tell him the truth. And with Dani, I'm not solving it this week. I'm asking her to pick a night before Easter so we can finish that conversation.

WES: That's patience. Not tonight, but it gets a night. And on the review, waiting isn't kindness. He just finds out later, with less time to fix it.

## SEGMENT 7 · This week's challenge

WES: So here's your challenge this week. Pick one thing you've been waiting on. Just one. Put a day and a time on it. A real day, not "sometime this week." Then tell one man. Text him, send a voice memo, whatever. "Wednesday at seven, I'm calling my brother."

JONATHON: And if you're married, ask your wife one question: "Is there something you've been waiting on me for?" Pick a calm night, not the end of a bad day. She might have a list. Don't defend anything. Just pick one thing off of it and put a day on it.

WES: If you're single, ask your closest friend, "What have I been putting off that you've noticed?" You'll be surprised how fast he answers.

WES: Friday I'll tell you whether I made the call. Until then: one thing, one day, one time. Tell one man.

JONATHON: Then go make the call, and tell us how it went at walksteadfast.com, and find us everywhere at @steadfastwalk.
