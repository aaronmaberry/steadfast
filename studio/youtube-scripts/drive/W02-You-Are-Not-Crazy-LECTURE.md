## OPEN

[COLD OPEN. No music under. Max seated at the table, single camera, direct to lens. Lower third: Max | Steadfast.]

Let me start with a question. Answer it honestly, even if you only answer it in your own head.

Have you ever felt like you were the only man in the room who actually wanted to do this right?

Not perfectly. Right. You want to love your wife well. You want your kids to learn what a man is by watching you. You want to work hard and tell the truth and not turn into the guy who quietly checked out somewhere around forty-one. You want to follow Jesus like it costs something, because it does.

And then you look around.

You see a culture that can't decide whether men are the problem or whether "man" is even a real category anymore. You see men your age who walked out on their families and seem to be doing fine. You see churches that will happily put you on the parking team but have never once told you what you are supposed to be as a man. You see a hundred voices online selling a hundred versions of masculinity, and half of them contradict the other half, and most of them want your credit card.

And somewhere in the middle of all that, a quiet thought shows up. Maybe I'm the one who's off. Maybe I'm making too much of this. Maybe wanting this is old-fashioned, or naive, or just a little bit crazy.

[Beat. Lean in.]

Here is what I am going to tell you for the next thirty minutes. You are not crazy.

You are not crazy for feeling like the ground moved. It did. You are not crazy for wanting a clear definition of what a man is. You need one, and you were built for one. You are not crazy for sensing that faithfulness is costing you while the other road seems to be paying out. That is exactly how it looks from where you're standing. And you are not crazy for suspecting you were made for more than drifting. You were.

My name is Max. This is Steadfast. Twenty-four weeks, building men who don't move. This is week two.

[Lower third: Week 2 of 24 | You Are Not Crazy]

If you were here last week, you know we are building all of this on one sentence. We will say it every week, at the open and at the close, until you can say it in your sleep and, more to the point, until you can live it awake.

A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward.

Next week we take that sentence apart one mark at a time. But before we can build anything on it, I have to deal with something. Because a lot of you heard that sentence and something in you stood up. And then you went back to your life, and by Wednesday the noise was louder than the sentence.

So this week is about the noise. It's about a disorientation that most men feel and almost nobody names. And it's about where a man goes to get his feet back under him.

## CLAIM

[Straight to camera. Slow down.]

Here is the claim for this week. I'll say it twice.

The confusion you feel about manhood is not a defect in you. It is the fruit of a world that lost the definition. And God has never lost it.

Again. The confusion you feel about manhood is not a defect in you. It is the fruit of a world that lost the definition. And God has never lost it.

That claim says two things at once, and you need both.

First, the confusion is real. I am not going to tell you it's all in your head. It isn't. There are real forces working on you: a culture with no shared picture of what a man is, a marketplace that makes money off your insecurity, and an enemy who has been trying to get men to sit down and stay quiet since the third chapter of Genesis. If you feel pulled in six directions, it's because you are being pulled in six directions.

Second, the confusion is not the last word. There is a fixed point. There is a place a man can stand where the ground does not move. And the rest of this hour is about a man who found it the hard way.

## TEXT

[On screen: Psalm 73. Max opens Bible. Read slowly, unhurried.]

Open to Psalm 73. If you don't have a Bible in reach, pull it up on your phone. That is the one time in this lecture I will tell you to look at your phone.

Psalm 73 was written by a man named Asaph. Asaph was not a rookie. He was a worship leader appointed under King David. He knew the songs. He had led thousands of men in the praise of God. He was, by every outward measure, a professional. And Psalm 73 is Asaph telling you, on the record, that he almost lost his footing.

Listen to how he opens.

[Read.]

"Truly God is good to Israel, to those who are pure in heart. But as for me, my feet had almost stumbled, my steps had nearly slipped. For I was envious of the arrogant when I saw the prosperity of the wicked."

Then he describes what he saw. Verse 4: "They have no pangs until death; their bodies are fat and sleek." Verse 5: "They are not in trouble as others are." Verse 6: "Pride is their necklace." Verse 8: "They scoff and speak with malice." Verse 9: "They set their mouths against the heavens, and their tongue struts through the earth." Verse 11: "And they say, 'How can God know?'" Verse 12: "Behold, these are the wicked; always at ease, they increase in riches."

Now hear what he almost concluded. Verse 13.

"All in vain have I kept my heart clean and washed my hands in innocence. For all the day long I have been stricken and rebuked every morning."

Hold that verse. We're coming back to it.

Verses 15 and 16: "If I had said, 'I will speak thus,' I would have betrayed the generation of your children. But when I thought how to understand this, it seemed to me a wearisome task,"

And then verse 17. The hinge of the whole psalm.

"until I went into the sanctuary of God; then I discerned their end."

Now he sees it. Verses 18 through 20: the arrogant are on slippery ground, swept away in a moment, like a dream you wake up from. Then he turns the light on himself. Verses 21 and 22:

"When my soul was embittered, when I was pricked in heart, I was brutish and ignorant; I was like a beast toward you."

And then the words I want you to carry out of this room. Verses 23 through 26:

"Nevertheless, I am continually with you; you hold my right hand. You guide me with your counsel, and afterward you will receive me to glory. Whom have I in heaven but you? And there is nothing on earth that I desire besides you. My flesh and my heart may fail, but God is the strength of my heart and my portion forever."

[Close the Bible. Look up.]

A worship leader. A veteran. A man who knew better. And his feet nearly went out from under him because he looked at the world and it did not add up.

I want you to notice the shape of this psalm before we walk through it, because the shape is the lesson. It starts with a true statement about God. It drops into an honest confession about the man. It bottoms out in a lie he almost said out loud. It turns on a single location. And it ends with the man's hand in the hand of God. True, honest, low, turn, held. That is the road back for a disoriented man. Asaph walked it. You can walk it.

Men, if Asaph can slip, you can slip. And if Asaph can get his feet back, so can you. Let's walk it in four movements.

## MOVEMENTS

### Movement 1: The slip is real

[On screen: "My feet had almost stumbled." Psalm 73:2]

Start with what Asaph does in verse 2. He tells the truth about where his feet are.

Notice he doesn't clean it up. He doesn't say, "I went through a season of mild spiritual questioning." He says my feet almost went out from under me. That is the first act of a steadfast man. Not strength. Honesty. A man who will not admit he is slipping cannot be steadied, because he will not reach for anything.

So let me describe the slip the way it actually happens today, and you tell me if this is you.

A boy grows up and nobody hands him a definition. Nobody sits him down at thirteen or eighteen and says, "Here is what a man is. Here is what you are aiming at. Here is how you'll know when you've arrived." Instead he gets scripts, and the scripts contradict each other. Be strong, but don't be intimidating. Lead, but don't be controlling. Provide, but don't be a workaholic. Protect, but understand that your instinct to protect is part of the problem. Be a man, but we are not going to tell you what that means, and if you ask, we'll assume something is wrong with you for asking.

He hits twenty-five and he's guessing. He hits thirty-five and he's still guessing, but now he has a wife and a mortgage and a two-year-old, so he's guessing with stakes. He hits fifty and he has decided that guessing is all there is. And he calls that maturity.

[Beat.]

Now add what Asaph adds in verse 3. He looks over at the men who don't care about any of this, and they look fine. Better than fine. Verse 4, no pangs. Verse 5, not in trouble like everybody else. Verse 12, always at ease, and they keep getting richer.

You know this man. He left his wife and he's got a boat. He cuts every corner at work and he just got promoted. He mocks everything you hold sacred and he sleeps like a baby. And you, the man who is trying, wake up at three in the morning with your jaw clenched.

Here is what I want you to see. Asaph was envious. He admits it. And the envy is what made his feet slip, not the facts. The facts were true: the arrogant were prospering. But envy took a true fact and turned it into a false conclusion. That's how the slip works. It doesn't usually start with a lie. It starts with something true that you look at for too long from the wrong angle.

So why does a man in this situation feel like he's the crazy one?

Isaiah gives the diagnosis. Isaiah 5:20: "Woe to those who call evil good and good evil, who put darkness for light and light for darkness, who put bitter for sweet and sweet for bitter." If you live in a place where light and dark have been relabeled, you are going to feel dizzy. That dizziness is not insanity. It is evidence that your inner ear still works.

The book of Judges puts it in one sentence. Judges 21:25: "In those days there was no king in Israel. Everyone did what was right in his own eyes." That is not just ancient history. That is a description of an age. No king means no definition. No definition means every man is his own referee. And when every man is his own referee, the man who wants an actual standard looks like the odd one.

You are not the odd one. You are the one who noticed.

So here is your first move, and it's Asaph's move. Say it plainly. To God, out loud, in your truck if you have to. "My feet have nearly slipped." That is not a confession of failure. It is a confession of condition. And it is the first honest thing a lot of men have said to God in years.

### Movement 2: The lie of "in vain"

[On screen: "All in vain have I kept my heart clean." Psalm 73:13]

Now go to verse 13, because this is where the slip becomes dangerous.

"All in vain have I kept my heart clean and washed my hands in innocence."

Men, that is the single most dangerous sentence a man can start to believe: it doesn't pay to do this right.

Let me show you where it shows up, because it rarely shows up as a sentence. It shows up as a mood.

It shows up in a marriage where you keep showing up, keep initiating, keep taking the hit, and she seems unmoved, and one night you think, why am I even doing this?

It shows up at work where you refuse to fudge the numbers and the guy who fudged them just got the bonus, and you think, what exactly is integrity buying me?

It shows up in the fight for purity, where you keep saying no, and the whole world is laughing at you for saying no, and you think, who is this even for?

It shows up at church, where you serve and give and pray and nobody notices, and you think, maybe the guys who sleep in on Sunday have it figured out.

That is the "in vain" lie. And it is a lie, but I want you to notice that Asaph doesn't call it a lie yet. In verse 13 he's still inside it. What stops him is verse 15.

"If I had said, 'I will speak thus,' I would have betrayed the generation of your children."

He caught himself because of who was listening.

[Slow down.]

Brother, somebody is listening to you. Your son is listening. Your daughter is watching how a man talks about faithfulness when faithfulness is expensive. That twenty-four-year-old at work who thinks you're the only honest man in the building is listening. The kid at church who doesn't have a dad is listening to how you talk about God when God seems slow.

When a man of God announces, even with his mood, even with his shrug, that doing this right is pointless, he doesn't slip alone. He takes the generation behind him down with him. Asaph knew that. It stopped his mouth. Let it stop yours.

Now let me actually answer the lie, because I don't want to just tell you not to say it. I want you to know why it's false.

The "in vain" lie is a math error. It's counting the wrong columns. It counts money. It counts ease. It counts applause and followers and the boat in the driveway. It does not count a single thing God counts. It doesn't count a wife who is safe. It doesn't count a son who knows what a man is because he lived with one. It doesn't count a clear conscience at two in the morning. It doesn't count the reward that God himself is holding for the man who did it right when nobody was clapping.

Paul answers Psalm 73:13 directly. 1 Corinthians 15:58: "Therefore, my beloved brothers, be steadfast, immovable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, knowing that in the Lord your labor is not in vain."

Not in vain. That is the direct rebuttal. Asaph says "in vain." God says "not in vain." And this whole series is named after the word Paul uses right before that. Steadfast.

One more thing on this movement, because Asaph sees it once he gets his footing. Verses 18 to 20. The prosperity of the arrogant is real, but it is temporary. Slippery places. Swept away in a moment. "Like a dream when one awakes." The man with the boat is living in a dream, and dreams end. You are not crazy for choosing what lasts over what glitters. You are doing the math right.

### Movement 3: The sanctuary

[On screen: "Until I went into the sanctuary of God." Psalm 73:17]

Verse 16. "When I thought how to understand this, it seemed to me a wearisome task."

I want you to hear that, because a lot of you are trying to think your way out of the disorientation. You're consuming more. More podcasts, more takes, more arguments about masculinity, more men on the internet telling you what a man is. And you are more tired and less clear than when you started. Asaph tried that. He called it wearisome. You cannot out-think disorientation by staying in the place that disoriented you.

Verse 17. "Until I went into the sanctuary of God; then I discerned their end."

The sanctuary. For Asaph, that was the physical place of God's presence, where God's people gathered and God's Word was read and God was worshiped. For you, it is the same three things in your own life: the Word of God open in front of you, the presence of God sought in prayer, and the people of God in an actual room on an actual Sunday.

Now notice what changed and what didn't. The facts didn't change. The arrogant were still prospering when Asaph walked out of the sanctuary. What changed was his sight. "Then I discerned their end." He could see where it was all going. Perspective is a function of location. Where you stand determines what you see. Stand in the culture's noise and you will see what the culture sees. Stand in the sanctuary and you will see the end of the story.

[Beat.]

Picture a man driving home late on a two-lane road in heavy fog. He turns his high beams on because that seems like the strong thing to do, and it makes it worse. The light just bounces back at him. What actually gets him home? He slows down, drops his eyes, and finds the painted line on the edge of the road. That line doesn't move. It doesn't care about the fog. It was put there before he got there, and it will be there after he passes.

That is the Word of God for a disoriented man. It is not more light bouncing off the fog. It is the painted line.

Paul says it this way in Romans 12:2: "Do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewal of your mind, that by testing you may discern what is the will of God, what is good and acceptable and perfect." Renewal of the mind. Discernment. That's Asaph's word: discerned. The renewed mind is a mind that has been in the sanctuary.

So what does the sanctuary look like on a Tuesday for a man with a job and kids? Three things, and I'll get specific in the application. The Word before the phone. The Lord's Day in the room, not on the couch. And a table with men who are aiming at the same target. That last one is what Steadfast is. It's a sanctuary with chairs.

Let me say one hard thing here. Some of you have not been in a room with God's people in months, and you've told yourself it's because the church is confused too. Maybe your church is confused. Plenty are. But Asaph didn't get his sight back by staying home and being right about everyone else. He went in. Find a church that opens the Book and go in. You have been trying to see straight from the wrong location. Move.

### Movement 4: Held

[On screen: "Nevertheless, I am continually with you; you hold my right hand." Psalm 73:23]

Now the last movement, and the one I most want you to remember.

Verses 21 and 22. Asaph comes clean. "When my soul was embittered, when I was pricked in heart, I was brutish and ignorant; I was like a beast toward you." He doesn't excuse the slip. He owns it. We'll spend a whole week on that in a few weeks, because owning it is one of the four marks. But look at the very next word, because it might be the most important word in the psalm.

"Nevertheless."

Not "therefore." Therefore would mean, I was like a beast, therefore you left. No. Nevertheless. I was like a beast toward you, nevertheless, "I am continually with you; you hold my right hand."

Watch the direction of the grip. Asaph does not say, I held on to you. He says you hold my right hand.

Picture a father at a busy crosswalk with a three-year-old. Whose grip is keeping that child alive? Not the child's. The child gets distracted, sees something across the street, lets go entirely. The father's grip does not depend on the child's. That is the picture. Your steadiness in a disorienting world does not finally rest on how well you hold on to God. It rests on the fact that he is holding you.

Then, verse 24: "You guide me with your counsel, and afterward you will receive me to glory." Direction now, destination later. That is the greater reward showing up in a psalm about disorientation. Asaph looked at the arrogant and saw their end. Then he looked at himself and saw his end, and it was glory. That's the fourth mark, brother. A man who expects God's reward can watch other men prosper without losing his mind.

Verse 25: "Whom have I in heaven but you? And there is nothing on earth that I desire besides you." Do you see what happened to the envy? It's gone. Not because the boat disappeared. Because his desire got reordered. When God becomes the thing you want, the things other men have stop running your heart.

And verse 26. Say it with me if you know it. "My flesh and my heart may fail, but God is the strength of my heart and my portion forever."

[Beat.]

Now one more thing, because I know some of you feel this part hardest. You feel alone in it. Like you are the last one who cares.

There was a prophet who felt that. Elijah, 1 Kings 19. After the greatest victory of his life, he's hiding in a cave, and he tells God, "I, even I only, am left." He says it twice. And God's answer, verse 18: "Yet I will leave seven thousand in Israel." Seven thousand. Elijah thought he was the last man standing and he was off by seven thousand.

You are off by more than that. There are men watching this right now who want exactly what you want. There are men in your church who have never said it out loud because they thought they were the only one. Steadfast exists because there are more of you than you think, and it is time you found each other.

You are not crazy. You are not alone. And you are held.

## APPLICATION

[Shift posture. Practical register. On screen: Name it / Do this week / Tell a brother / Carry this]

Every week we are going to close the same way. Four moves. Name it. Do this week. Tell a brother. Carry this. Here they are for week two.

### Name it

Name the slip. Where exactly have your feet nearly gone? Be specific, because "I've been struggling" is not a name.

Is it envy? A specific man whose life you keep measuring yours against? Is it cynicism, the low hum of "none of this matters"? Is it the "in vain" sentence in one specific area: your marriage, your work, your fight for purity, your church? Is it isolation, the belief that you are the last one who cares?

Write it down. One sentence. "My feet have nearly slipped here." Then say it to God, out loud, before you go to sleep tonight.

### Do this week

Three things. They are not hard. They are specific.

One. Sanctuary before screen. For the next seven days, read Psalm 73 before you touch your phone in the morning. The whole thing. It takes four minutes. Read it seven times this week and I promise you it will start reading you.

Two. Be in the room on Sunday. Not the livestream. The room. Asaph went into the sanctuary; he didn't stream it. Sit near the front. Sing like a man who means it.

Three. Write Psalm 73:23 through 26 on an index card and put it where you shave, or on your dashboard, or in the visor of your truck. Read it every time you see it. "Nevertheless, I am continually with you; you hold my right hand."

### Tell a brother

This week you send one text to one man. Here's the text. You can steal it word for word.

"I've been feeling like I'm the only one trying to take this seriously. I don't think I actually am. Can we get coffee this week?"

Then set the time. Don't leave it at "sometime." If you don't have a man to send that to, that is your assignment for the week: find one. Look around your church. Look at the man two rows up who sings like he means it. That's your guy.

### Carry this

One line for the week. Say it when the noise gets loud.

"You are not crazy. You are not alone. You are held."

Say it in the parking lot. Say it at three in the morning. Say it over your kids' beds. You are not crazy. You are not alone. You are held.

## CLOSE

[Return to seated, direct to camera. Slow.]

Let me land this.

Asaph was a professional worshiper who nearly lost his footing because he looked at the world and it didn't add up. He told the truth about the slip. He almost swallowed the lie that faithfulness is pointless, and the thought of the men behind him stopped his mouth. He went into the sanctuary and got his sight back. And he discovered that the whole time he was slipping, God never once let go of his hand.

That is your story too, if you'll let it be.

The confusion is real. You are not imagining it. But the confusion is not yours to solve by thinking harder or scrolling longer. It is solved by standing in the right place, with the right Book open, next to the right men, held by a God who has never once been confused about what a man is.

Here is what he says a man is. Same sentence every week.

A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward.

Next week we take that sentence apart. Four marks, one at a time. I'll show you where they come from, where the first man dropped every one of them, and the one Man who lived every one of them perfectly. If you don't want to miss it, subscribe and hit the bell, because this is a twenty-four week build and every week stands on the one before it.

[Beat.]

Stand firm. Act like men. Be strong.

I'll see you next week.
