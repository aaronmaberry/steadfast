## OPEN

[COLD OPEN. No music under. Max seated at the table, direct to lens. Lower third: Max | Steadfast.]

I'm going to read you a list of sentences. I want you to count how many of them you have said in the last year. Count honestly.

"When work slows down."

"After the holidays."

"When the kids are a little older."

"Once I get my own stuff together."

"I'm waiting for the right time."

"I'm praying about it."

"I'm waiting on God."

"I just need a little more information."

"I'm going to let her bring it up."

"That's really more the pastor's job."

[Beat.]

How many? Three? Six? All ten?

Here's the thing about that list. Every single sentence on it can be true. There are seasons when work really is too heavy. There are decisions you really should pray about. There are things that really are somebody else's job. I'm not going to stand here and tell you that patience is a sin or that prayer is an excuse.

But I am going to tell you this. Every sentence on that list can also be a mat. A place where a man lies down and calls it wisdom. And most of the men I know are not lying on a mat because they're lazy. They're lying on it because they have a very good reason. And they've had that reason for a very long time.

My name is Max. This is Steadfast. Twenty-four weeks, building men who don't move. This is week four.

[Lower third: Week 4 of 24 | Stop Waiting]

Two weeks ago I told you that you're not crazy. Last week I handed you the level, four marks that tell the truth about a man. Here's the sentence. You should be able to say it with me by now.

A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward.

Today we take the first mark and go all the way down. Rejects passivity. And I want to do it through a man who lay beside a pool for thirty-eight years with the best excuse you've ever heard, until Jesus walked up and asked him one question.

## CLAIM

[Straight to camera. Slow down.]

Here's the claim for this week. Twice.

Passivity is not doing nothing. Passivity is waiting for something to happen that you were supposed to make happen. And Jesus does not meet waiting men with more time. He meets them with a question and a command.

Again. Passivity is not doing nothing. Passivity is waiting for something to happen that you were supposed to make happen. And Jesus does not meet waiting men with more time. He meets them with a question and a command.

I want to be precise about the first part, because most men hear the word passivity and picture a guy on a couch. That's not the passive man. The passive man has a job. He's busy. He answers his emails. He mows his lawn. His passivity is not in what he does. It's in the one or two things he keeps not doing. The conversation he keeps not having. The decision he keeps not making. The step he keeps not taking toward the thing he already knows God wants from him.

Last week we saw Adam standing right there while the serpent lied to his wife. "Her husband who was with her." He didn't leave. He didn't run. He was present and silent. That's passivity in its purest form: a man in the room, seeing what needs to be done, and waiting.

Waiting for what? That's the question this whole lecture is about.

## TEXT

[On screen: John 5:1-9. Max opens Bible.]

John chapter 5. Jesus has gone up to Jerusalem for a feast. There's a pool near the Sheep Gate called Bethesda, with five covered porches around it, and lying on those porches is a crowd of invalids. Blind men. Lame men. Paralyzed men. And there's a belief attached to the pool: when the water is stirred, the first one in gets healed.

Verse 5.

[Read.]

"One man was there who had been an invalid for thirty-eight years. When Jesus saw him lying there and knew that he had already been there a long time, he said to him, 'Do you want to be healed?'"

Hold that question. We'll spend a whole movement on it.

Verse 7. "The sick man answered him, 'Sir, I have no one to put me into the pool when the water is stirred up, and while I am going another steps down before me.'"

Notice he didn't answer the question. Jesus asked yes or no. He gave a paragraph.

Verse 8. "Jesus said to him, 'Get up, take up your bed, and walk.'"

Verse 9. "And at once the man was healed, and he took up his bed and walked. Now that day was the Sabbath."

Then skip down to verse 14, because there's a detail I don't want you to miss. "Afterward Jesus found him in the temple and said to him, 'See, you are well! Sin no more, that nothing worse may happen to you.'"

[Close the Bible. Look up.]

Thirty-eight years. Let me put that in your terms. Thirty-eight years ago, some of you weren't born. Some of you were in elementary school. This man has been lying beside a pool since before some of you existed. He has a system: the pool. He has a story: I have no one. He has a clock: thirty-eight years. And he has, I promise you, made a kind of peace with it. You don't lie somewhere for thirty-eight years without it becoming home.

And then a Man walks up who does not care about the system, does not accept the story, and does not add a single day to the clock.

Four movements. The shapes of waiting. The question. The command. And then a prophet who said the same thing to a whole nation.

## MOVEMENTS

### Movement 1: The shapes of waiting

[On screen: "an invalid for thirty-eight years" John 5:5]

Look at what this man had built around his waiting, because every passive man has the same three things.

He had a system. The pool. Bethesda had a whole theology attached to it: the water stirs, the first one in gets healed. So the man's entire life is organized around a mechanism that is going to fix him someday. He's not doing nothing. He's positioned. He's near the water. He's watching for the ripple. Ask him what he's doing with his life and he'd say, "I'm waiting for my healing." It sounds almost spiritual.

He had a story. Verse 7: "I have no one." And then: "while I am going another steps down before me." Two-part excuse. Part one, I'm alone. Part two, other people are faster. And here's what makes it a powerful story: both parts are probably true. He probably didn't have anyone. Other people probably did beat him to the water. A good excuse is never a lie. That's why it works.

And he had a clock. Thirty-eight years. A clock that just kept running while the system didn't deliver and the story stayed true.

And notice one more thing. He wasn't alone on that porch. Verse 3 says a multitude of invalids lay there. Passivity is rarely a solo project. A waiting man finds other waiting men, and they agree with each other. Nobody on that porch was going to look at him and say, "Thirty-eight years, brother. Maybe the pool isn't the plan." They were all watching the same water. If every man you spend time with has the same story you do, you are not in a friendship. You are on a porch.

Now let me show you the same three things in a man with a job and a truck.

The system: "When work slows down, I'll start leading my family spiritually." Work is the pool. He's positioned. He's watching for the ripple. Work never slows down.

The story: "My wife is better at the emotional stuff." Or, "I didn't have a dad who showed me this." Or, "Our church doesn't really have anything for men." Every one of those can be true. And every one of them is a mat.

The clock: he's been saying this since his oldest was three. His oldest is eleven.

[Beat.]

Solomon watched this man. Ecclesiastes 11:4: "He who observes the wind will not sow, and he who regards the clouds will not reap." The farmer who waits for perfect weather never plants. Not because he's lazy. Because he's a weather expert. He can tell you exactly why today isn't the day. He's very informed. And his field is empty.

Two verses later, verse 6: "In the morning sow your seed, and at evening withhold not your hand, for you do not know which will prosper, this or that." You don't know. You're not going to know. Sow anyway.

Proverbs 20:4: "The sluggard does not plow in the autumn; he will seek at harvest and have nothing." Notice when the sluggard shows up. He shows up at harvest. He wants the result. He just didn't want to be there in the season when the result was being made. That's the passive man at his daughter's wedding wondering why they're not close. He wanted the harvest. He skipped the autumn.

So here is the diagnostic for movement one. Ask yourself: what is my pool? What is the mechanism I'm quietly counting on to fix the thing I'm not moving on? What is my story, the true-sounding sentence I use every time somebody gets close to asking about it? And how long has my clock been running?

Say the number. Out loud. However many years. Say it.

### Movement 2: The question

[On screen: "Do you want to be healed?" John 5:6]

Now the question. Verse 6. Jesus sees him. Jesus knows he's been there a long time. And Jesus says, "Do you want to be healed?"

The first time you read that, it sounds almost cruel. Of course he wants to be healed. He's been lying by a healing pool for thirty-eight years. What kind of question is that?

It is exactly the right question. Because after thirty-eight years, wanting is not obvious anymore.

Here is what happens to a man who waits long enough. The mat stops being where he is and starts being who he is. His whole identity gets built around the condition. His friends are the other men on the porch. His daily routine is the pool. His story about himself is "the one who has no one." If Jesus heals him, he loses all of it. He has to get up and figure out who he is standing. He has to go find work. He has to stop being the man everyone feels sorry for and start being a man people expect things from.

Do you want that? It's a real question.

Brother, some of you have been passive in one area so long that the passivity has become comfortable. The marriage that's cold but stable. You've made peace with it. Fixing it would mean conversations you don't want to have. The career you've been "waiting on God" about for six years. You've made peace with it. Moving would mean risk. The spiritual leadership of your home that you handed to your wife a decade ago. You've made peace with it. Taking it back would mean being bad at something in front of your kids.

Do you want to be healed? Not, do you wish things were different. Do you want it. Because want has legs. Want does things.

Proverbs 13:4: "The soul of the sluggard craves and gets nothing, while the soul of the diligent is richly supplied." Do you see the difference between craving and wanting? The sluggard craves. He lies there and wishes. He has a very rich inner life of wanting things to be different. And he gets nothing. Because craving is wanting without a body.

Now look at how the man answers. Verse 7. He doesn't say yes. He explains. "Sir, I have no one." Jesus asked about his want. He answered with his story. That is the tell of a passive man: ask him a yes-or-no question about what he wants and he'll give you a paragraph about why he can't have it.

If your wife asked you tonight, "Do you want to lead this family spiritually?" and your honest first sentence starts with "Well, the thing is," you've just answered. Not the question she asked. The one Jesus asked.

[Slow down.]

So before you go one step further in this lecture, answer it. In the one area you named a minute ago. Do you want to be healed? Yes or no. No paragraph. No "the thing is." If the answer is no, be honest about that, because at least now you're telling the truth, and God can work with a man who tells the truth. If the answer is yes, then the next movement is for you.

### Movement 3: The command

[On screen: "Get up, take up your bed, and walk." John 5:8]

Verse 8. Jesus does not argue with the story. He doesn't say, "Actually, I'll be your someone; let me carry you to the water." He doesn't fix the pool. He doesn't rebuke the faster men. He completely ignores the system and the story, and he speaks directly to the man's body.

"Get up, take up your bed, and walk."

Three commands. Three verbs. Every one of them matters.

Get up. That's the vertical move. That's the first move, and it's the one that costs the most, because it's the one where you find out if it's real. Paul uses the same language to the church in Ephesians 5:14: "Awake, O sleeper, and arise from the dead." Getting up is not a plan. It's not a goal. It's not a resolution you write in a journal. It's your body changing position. For you, this week, it's a phone call. It's walking into a room. It's opening your mouth at the dinner table. Before the healing was visible, before he felt anything in his legs, the command was "get up." Obedience comes before the evidence. You do not get to feel the strength first and then stand. You stand on the word, and the strength meets you on the way up.

Picture the man whose mat is a conversation with his wife he has been avoiding for two years. Getting up, for him, is not fixing the marriage tonight. It is one sentence after the kids are down: "I've been avoiding something with you, and I don't want to avoid it anymore. Can we talk tomorrow night?" That's it. That's standing. His legs will feel like they don't work. He says it anyway. And I promise you the strength to have the actual conversation tomorrow night will be there in a way it never was while he was lying down.

Take up your bed. This is my favorite part. The bed is the thing that has been carrying him for thirty-eight years. Now he carries it. The mat that was his infrastructure becomes his luggage. That's what happens to your excuse when you finally move. "I have no one" was the reason he couldn't. Now the fact that he did it alone is the proof that he could. Your story doesn't disappear when you stop waiting. It gets flipped. "I didn't have a dad who showed me this" becomes "I didn't have a dad who showed me this, and I'm doing it anyway." Roll it up. Put it under your arm. It goes with you now, but it doesn't hold you anymore.

Walk. Not leap. Not sprint. Not run a marathon by Friday. Walk. Passivity's favorite trick when it finally gets confronted is to demand a heroic response. "If I'm going to lead my family, I need a whole plan. I need a curriculum. I need to fix everything." No. Walk. One step, then the next one, in a direction. The man didn't need to know where he was going. He needed to move his feet. Sustained motion in the right direction is what walking is. That's all Jesus asked.

Now, two details. Verse 9: "Now that day was the Sabbath." If you read the rest of the chapter, religious men see him carrying his bed and they're furious. Not that he's healed. That he's carrying something on the Sabbath. Here's what I want you to hear: when you get up off the mat, some people are going to be bothered. Some of them will be religious people. Some of them will be men still on the porch who liked you better lying down. When a passive man starts moving, it exposes everybody who isn't. Expect it.

And verse 14. Where does Jesus find him afterward? In the temple. The man who lay by a pool for thirty-eight years is now standing in the house of God. That's where getting up leads. Not to a better version of your old life. To a different location entirely.

[Beat.]

James says something that turns this whole thing from advice into a warning. James 4:17: "So whoever knows the right thing to do and fails to do it, for him it is sin." Not, for him it is a missed opportunity. Not, for him it is a growth area. Sin. Passivity is not neutral. Adam's silence in the garden wasn't neutral. Your waiting is not neutral. If you know the right thing to do, the clock is not on your side. It's the evidence against you.

### Movement 4: Consider your ways

[On screen: "Is it a time for you yourselves to dwell in your paneled houses, while this house lies in ruins?" Haggai 1:4]

Now I want to show you the same message delivered not to one man on a mat but to an entire nation of men on a mat. Haggai chapter 1.

The setting. God's people have come home from exile. They started rebuilding the temple, God's house, and then they stopped. Opposition, discouragement, life. And for about sixteen years, the foundation just sat there. Sixteen years of "not yet." And in those sixteen years, they didn't do nothing. Watch what they did.

Haggai 1:2: "Thus says the LORD of hosts: These people say the time has not yet come to rebuild the house of the LORD."

There's the story. "The time has not yet come." It's the national version of "when work slows down."

Verse 4: "Is it a time for you yourselves to dwell in your paneled houses, while this house lies in ruins?"

Paneled houses. They weren't idle. They'd been busy. They'd been building. They'd been improving their own homes, finishing them out, paneling the walls. All the energy that should have gone into God's house went into their comfort. And God's question is brutal: it's a time for that, but not for this?

Brother, let me ask you the same question. What have you been paneling while your house lies in ruins? Some of you have built an impressive career while your marriage sits at the foundation stage. Some of you have a garage, a truck, a golf game, a fantasy league, a home theater, a body, all finished out beautifully, and your kids have never once heard you pray out loud. You are not passive. You are extremely active. You are just active on the paneled house.

Verse 5: "Now, therefore, thus says the LORD of hosts: Consider your ways." And he says it again in verse 7. Consider your ways. Stop and look at where your energy has actually gone. Not where you meant it to go. Where it went.

And then verse 6, the results of sixteen years on the mat: "You have sown much, and harvested little. You eat, but you never have enough; you drink, but you never have your fill." Some of you know that feeling exactly. You work so hard and it never adds up to anything, and you can't figure out why. Haggai can. You built the wrong house.

Then verse 8, the command, and it's the same three verbs Jesus gave the man by the pool, just in construction language: "Go up to the hills and bring wood and build the house." Go up. Bring. Build. Get up. Take up. Walk.

[Beat.]

Now here's the part I love. Verse 12, the people listened. Verse 14: "And the LORD stirred up the spirit of Zerubbabel," the governor, "and the spirit of Joshua," the priest, "and the spirit of all the remnant of the people. And they came and worked on the house." Sixteen years of waiting, broken in one chapter. And then chapter 2, verse 4, God says: "Be strong. Work, for I am with you, declares the LORD of hosts."

Work, for I am with you. Do you hear what that does to "I'm waiting on God"? Some of you have been waiting on God for a word, and the word has been "build" the entire time. He's not waiting for the right moment. He's waiting for you to pick up the wood.

Now let me be careful, because the Bible does tell men to wait on the Lord. Psalm 27:14: "Wait for the LORD; be strong, and let your heart take courage; wait for the LORD!" So how do you tell the difference between waiting on God and lying on a mat? Here's the test. Biblical waiting is what you do about the things God has not told you. Passivity is what you do about the things he has. You can wait on God for a job offer. You cannot wait on God to love your wife. You can wait on God about whether to move cities. You cannot wait on God about whether to open the Bible with your kids. He already spoke. If the thing you're "waiting on God" about is something he has plainly commanded, you are not waiting. You are disobeying slowly. And look at the posture of real waiting in that psalm: "be strong, and let your heart take courage." That is not a man lying down. That is a man standing at his post, doing everything he already knows to do, while he trusts God with the part he doesn't.

Proverbs 24 describes the alternative. Solomon walks past a field, and it's overgrown with thorns, nettles everywhere, the stone wall broken down. And the diagnosis, verse 33: "A little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to rest." Nobody decided to ruin that field. It was ruined by littles. A little more waiting. A little more information. A little more "after the holidays." That's how a house ends up in ruins with a man living right next to it in a paneled room.

Consider your ways. Then go get the wood.

## APPLICATION

[Shift posture. Practical register. On screen: Name it / Do this week / Tell a brother / Carry this]

Four moves. Same as every week.

### Name it

Name your mat. Not your general tendency. The specific thing. Fill in this sentence with real words: "I have been waiting to _____ until _____."

I have been waiting to talk to my wife about our marriage until things calm down. I have been waiting to lead a family devotion until I know the Bible better. I have been waiting to deal with my drinking until after the busy season. I have been waiting to confront my brother until he apologizes first. I have been waiting to go to the doctor until it gets worse. I have been waiting to join a church until I find the perfect one.

Both blanks. The thing, and the condition. Write it down. Then write the number of years next to it.

### Do this week

Three things.

One. The seventy-two hour rule. Whatever you just named, you take the first physical step within seventy-two hours of the end of this lecture. Not the whole thing. The first step. Make the call. Book the appointment. Say the first sentence to her. Buy the book and open it. Get up. You don't have to walk far. You have to stand.

Two. Kill one little. Find the "little sleep, little slumber" habit that has been quietly feeding your waiting. The forty-five minutes on the phone before bed. The third show. The Saturday morning that always disappears. Pick one. Replace it for seven days with something that builds the house.

Three. Consider your ways. Twenty minutes, pen and paper, no phone. Two columns. Left column: "Paneled house." Everywhere your energy actually went this year. Right column: "House in ruins." What has been sitting at the foundation stage. Look at the two columns. Pick one thing from the right column. That's the wood you go get.

### Tell a brother

Tell one man your mat sentence, both blanks and the number of years. Then tell him your seventy-two hour step. Then ask him to text you at hour seventy-two with four words: "Did you do it?" And when he texts, you answer honestly.

If you did the coffee from week two and the audit from week three, this is the next conversation. You're building something with that man. Keep going.

### Carry this

One line. It's not mine. It's the one Jesus said.

"Get up. Take up your bed. Walk."

Say it in the driveway before you go in the house. Say it when the story starts running in your head. Say it when you catch yourself watching the clouds. Get up. Take up your bed. Walk.

## CLOSE

[Return to seated, direct to camera. Slow.]

Let me land this.

A man lay beside a pool for thirty-eight years with a system, a story, and a clock. And he wasn't lazy. He was positioned. He was informed. He was waiting for something to happen that he had quietly decided was somebody else's job. And Jesus walked up and did not give him more time. He asked him if he actually wanted it. And then he told him to get up, pick up the thing that had been holding him, and walk.

A nation stood beside a foundation for sixteen years, paneling their own houses and saying "not yet." And God did not send a prophet to comfort them. He sent a prophet to say: consider your ways, go get the wood, and work, for I am with you.

Brother, the ripple you've been watching for is not coming. The right time is not on the calendar. The someone you've been waiting for is not walking up the porch. But the Man who told a paralyzed man to stand is speaking to you, right now, in whatever area you named. And he is not saying wait.

Same sentence every week. Say it with me.

A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward.

Next week, the second mark. It's called "Pick Up the Weight," and it's about what a man does with a problem that isn't his fault. Some of you have been standing next to one of those for a while. Subscribe and hit the bell so you don't miss it, because every week of this build stands on the one before it.

[Beat.]

Stand firm. Act like men. Be strong.

I'll see you next week.
