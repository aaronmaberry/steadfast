## OPEN

[COLD OPEN. No music under. Max seated at the table, direct to lens. Lower third: Max | Steadfast.]

There's a moment that happens in every group of men. You've been in it a hundred times.

Six boys on a rock above a lake in July. The water's cold and it's a long way down, and everybody's talking, and nobody's jumping. Everybody's waiting to see who goes first.

Eight men in a church basement. The leader says, "Who wants to open us in prayer?" Silence. Eight grown men studying the carpet. Everybody's waiting to see who goes first.

A family at Thanksgiving. Dad's drinking has gotten worse, and every adult at that table knows it, and every adult at that table has talked about it with every other adult except Dad. Everybody's waiting to see who goes first.

A husband and wife lying in the dark after a fight, backs to each other, both awake, both knowing the marriage needs a sentence that starts with "I'm sorry," and both waiting to see who goes first.

[Beat.]

Here's what I've learned about those moments. Everybody in the room knows what needs to happen. That's almost never the problem. The problem is that what needs to happen requires somebody to move before anyone else does, and there is a cost to moving first, and everybody can feel it.

That's what leadership is. Strip away the titles and the org charts and the personality tests. Leadership is the answer to one question: who goes first?

My name is Max. This is Steadfast. Twenty-four weeks, building men who don't move. This is week six.

[Lower third: Week 6 of 24 | Go First]

We've taken two marks apart so far. Stop waiting: rejects passivity. Pick up the weight: accepts responsibility. Today, the third. Here's the sentence. You know it by now.

A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward.

Leads courageously. Today I'm going to take you to a river at flood stage that would not move until somebody put his feet in it. And I'm going to show you the difference between a king sitting with six hundred men and his son climbing a cliff with one.

## CLAIM

[Straight to camera. Slow down.]

Here's the claim. Twice.

Courageous leadership is not a title, a personality, or a volume. It is the willingness to go first into what everyone else is waiting on, and to take them with you. And the river does not part for the man standing on the bank.

Again. Courageous leadership is not a title, a personality, or a volume. It is the willingness to go first into what everyone else is waiting on, and to take them with you. And the river does not part for the man standing on the bank.

Three things it is not. Let me clear those first.

Not a title. Some of you have the title. Husband. Father. Elder. Manager. "Head of the household." You can hold every one of those and lead nothing, because a title tells people what to call you. It doesn't tell them where you're going.

Not a personality. Some of you have already decided you're not a leader because you're quiet, or you don't like the front of the room, or you're not the guy with the big ideas at the meeting. Listen to me. Some of the most courageous leaders I have ever watched would rather die than give a speech. Leadership is not extroversion. It's direction.

Not a volume. The loudest man in the room is often the most frightened. Raising your voice is not leading. It's what a man does when he's lost the room and knows it.

What it is: going first, and taking people with you. Both halves. A man who goes first and leaves everyone behind is not a leader, he's a loner. A man who takes people with him but never goes first is a manager. A leader is the man who steps in the water while everyone else watches, and then stands there until they've all crossed.

## TEXT

[On screen: Joshua 3:7-17. Max opens Bible.]

Joshua chapter 3. Set the scene. Moses is dead. Joshua has the job. Israel has been in the wilderness for forty years and they're finally standing on the east bank of the Jordan River, and the promised land is on the other side. And there's a problem. Verse 15 tells you in a parenthesis: "the Jordan overflows all its banks throughout the time of harvest." It's flood season. This isn't a creek. It's a fast, wide, brown river, and there are a couple million people, with children and livestock, standing on the wrong side of it.

Now, forty years earlier, God had done this once already. Exodus 14. Moses stretched out his hand, the Red Sea split, and then the people walked through on dry ground. Sea first, then feet.

Watch what God does this time. Verse 7.

[Read.]

"The LORD said to Joshua, 'Today I will begin to exalt you in the sight of all Israel, that they may know that, as I was with Moses, so I will be with you. And as for you, command the priests who bear the ark of the covenant, "When you come to the brink of the waters of the Jordan, you shall stand still in the Jordan."'"

Then Joshua tells the people what's coming. Verse 13:

"And when the soles of the feet of the priests bearing the ark of the LORD, the Lord of all the earth, shall rest in the waters of the Jordan, the waters of the Jordan shall be cut off from flowing, and the waters coming down from above shall stand in one heap."

Hear the order. When the feet rest in the water, then the water stops. Not the other way around.

Verse 14. "So when the people set out from their tents to pass over the Jordan with the priests bearing the ark of the covenant before the people, and as soon as those bearing the ark had come as far as the Jordan, and the feet of the priests bearing the ark were dipped in the brink of the water," here's the parenthesis, "(now the Jordan overflows all its banks throughout the time of harvest), the waters coming down from above stood and rose up in a heap very far away."

Verse 16 finishes: the water downstream "were completely cut off. And the people passed over opposite Jericho."

And then verse 17, the verse I most want you to see.

"Now the priests bearing the ark of the covenant of the LORD stood firmly on dry ground in the midst of the Jordan, and all Israel was passing over on dry ground until all the nation finished passing over the Jordan."

[Close the Bible. Look up.]

Feet in the flood. Then the water moves. Then a handful of men stand in the middle of a riverbed, holding the presence of God, while an entire nation walks past them to the other side. And they don't move until the last one is across.

That is a complete picture of what it means to lead courageously. Four movements.

## MOVEMENTS

### Movement 1: Leadership is a direction, not a title

[On screen: "Jesus was walking ahead of them." Mark 10:32]

Before we go back into the river, I want to give you the simplest definition of leadership in the Bible. It's not in Joshua. It's in Mark.

Mark 10:32. "And they were on the road, going up to Jerusalem, and Jesus was walking ahead of them. And they were amazed, and those who followed were afraid."

Walking ahead of them. That's it. That's the definition. Jesus knows exactly what's in Jerusalem. He tells them in the next two verses: he'll be handed over, condemned, mocked, spit on, flogged, killed. And he's not behind them, pushing. He's not beside them, discussing. He's out front. His feet are going toward the hard thing before anyone else's are, and his men are following, scared to death, because the Man in front of them isn't stopping.

Leadership is where your feet go when it gets hard, and whether anyone can follow you there.

Now look at how God commissioned Joshua in chapter 1, because it tells you two things about what going first requires. Verse 6: "Be strong and courageous, for you shall cause this people to inherit the land." Notice the job. Not "you shall inherit the land." You shall cause this people to. Leading isn't about you getting across. It's about them getting across. And then verse 9: "Have I not commanded you? Be strong and courageous. Do not be frightened, and do not be dismayed, for the LORD your God is with you wherever you go."

Three times in four verses God tells Joshua to be courageous. Why would you tell a man three times not to be afraid? Because he was afraid. Courage is not the absence of fear. If you weren't afraid you wouldn't need courage. Courage is fear with its feet moving in the right direction.

[Beat.]

So what does this look like in a house? It looks like firsts. Who prays first at the table? Who apologizes first after a fight? Who is the first to bring up the money problem, the porn problem, the drinking problem, the "we haven't been to church in two months" problem? Who turns off his screen first? Who gives first, before the budget is comfortable? Who is the first to say to a sixteen-year-old, "I need to ask you a hard question, and I'm going to love you no matter what the answer is"?

The firsts of a home are the leadership of a home. You can hold the title "husband and father" and lose every single first to your wife, and then wonder why she seems tired and why your kids don't look to you. She's leading. Not because she took it. Because you didn't go.

Brother, where are your feet going this week that anyone in your house could follow?

### Movement 2: Feet in the water

[On screen: "the feet of the priests were dipped in the brink of the water" Joshua 3:15]

Now back to the river. I want you to see three things about those priests.

First, they stepped in before the evidence.

Remember, God reversed the order. At the Red Sea, the water split and then the people walked. At the Jordan, the feet went in and then the water stopped. Verse 13 is a promise, and a promise is not a result. When those men walked down the bank, the Jordan was still at flood stage. It was still cold and brown and fast. They were carrying a wooden box covered in gold, which is not something you want to drop in a river. And they had a word from God and nothing else. No evidence. No proof of concept. No sign the water was going to do anything but knock them down.

And they stepped in.

That's the first thing about going first. You don't get to see it work before you do it. The husband who says "I'm sorry" first doesn't know if she'll say it back. The father who starts praying at dinner doesn't know if his teenager will roll his eyes. The man who tells his small group "I'm struggling" doesn't know if the room will go cold. You act on the word, and the water moves after the feet do. Never before.

Second, they got wet first.

"Dipped in the brink of the water." Before the miracle, there was a moment when a few men had the Jordan River running over their sandals and nobody else did. The leader's feet are the only ones in the flood. That's the cost of going first, and there's no way around it. Somebody has to be cold and wet and uncertain for a second so that everyone else can walk on dry ground.

Some of you want to lead without ever getting wet. You want to inspire from the bank. You want to hand your wife a book about marriage instead of being the first to say the hard true sentence. You want to send your son to a youth group instead of being the first man to tell him what you struggled with at his age. You can't lead dry. If your feet have never been in it, nobody's following you across.

Third, and this is the one men skip: they stood there.

Verse 17. "The priests bearing the ark of the covenant of the LORD stood firmly on dry ground in the midst of the Jordan, and all Israel was passing over on dry ground until all the nation finished passing over."

They didn't cross first and celebrate on the other side. They stood in the middle of the riverbed, with a wall of water heaped up somewhere upstream, holding the ark, while every last person walked past them. Kids. Grandmothers. Sheep. The slow ones. The scared ones. And they didn't move until the last one was over.

That's what leading courageously actually costs. Not the first step. The standing. Anybody can have a brave moment. A leader stands in the riverbed for as long as it takes for the people he's responsible for to get across. The husband who starts the hard conversation and then stays in it for the two years it takes to rebuild trust. The father who goes first in confessing and then stands there while his son slowly, awkwardly, learns how to do the same. The man who starts the men's table at church and keeps showing up when it's three guys and cold coffee in month four.

[Slow down.]

And notice one more thing. What were they carrying? The ark. The presence of God. They went first, but they didn't go alone, and they didn't go on their own strength. Going first is not self-reliance. It's stepping into the flood carrying God with you, on his word, trusting that he'll do the part only he can do. The man who goes first without God is just reckless. The man who goes first with God is a leader.

### Movement 3: "It may be that the LORD will work"

[On screen: "It may be that the LORD will work for us." 1 Samuel 14:6]

Now I want to show you two men in the same army on the same day, because they show you what courageous leadership is and what it isn't.

1 Samuel 13 and 14. Israel is in trouble. The Philistines have taken the high ground at a pass, and Israel's army is so beaten down that chapter 13, verse 6 says "the people hid themselves in caves and in holes and in rocks and in tombs and in cisterns." That's your nation. In holes.

The king, Saul, is sitting at the edge of Gibeah with six hundred men. Chapter 14, verse 2. He's got the title. He's got the crown. He's got six hundred soldiers. And he's sitting.

His son Jonathan turns to the young man who carries his armor. Verse 1: "Come, let us go over to the Philistine garrison on the other side." And verse 6, the sentence I want you to memorize: "Come, let us go over to the garrison of these uncircumcised. It may be that the LORD will work for us, for nothing can hinder the LORD from saving by many or by few."

It may be. Did you hear that? Not "God told me we'll win." Not "I have a guarantee." It may be that the LORD will work for us. Jonathan does not have certainty. What he has is a conviction about the character of God: nothing can hinder the LORD. He knows who God is. He doesn't know what God will do. And he goes anyway.

That's courage. Certainty doesn't require courage. If you knew it would work, you wouldn't need to be brave. Courage is moving on what you know about God when you don't know how it's going to turn out.

Now look at the armor-bearer. Verse 7: "Do all that is in your heart. Do as you wish. Behold, I am with you heart and soul." One man goes first, and immediately, another man is with him heart and soul. That's how it works. Men follow men who go. The armor-bearer didn't follow the crown. The crown was sitting back at Gibeah. He followed the guy who was moving.

Verse 13: "Then Jonathan climbed up on his hands and feet, and his armor-bearer after him." On his hands and feet. Going first is not dignified. It's a man scrambling up a cliff face on all fours while the enemy watches. If you're waiting to lead until you can do it gracefully, you'll never lead. Jonathan climbed ugly.

And then watch what God does. Verse 15: "there was a panic in the camp." The earth quakes. The Philistines turn on each other. And verse 22, here's the part I love: "when all the men of Israel who had hidden themselves in the hill country of Ephraim heard that the Philistines were fleeing, they too followed hard after them in the battle."

The men in the holes came out.

[Beat.]

Brother, there are men in holes all around you. In your church. At your work. In your extended family. Men who know what a man is supposed to be and have decided it can't be done, so they've hidden in their careers and their hobbies and their screens. And they are not going to come out because somebody with a title tells them to. Saul had the title. Saul had six hundred men. Nobody came out for Saul.

They come out when one man goes first. When one man in the men's group says, "I'm going to be honest, I've been struggling with this," three other men suddenly find their voices. When one father starts praying with his kids at the table, the family two houses down hears about it and tries it. When one man climbs the cliff on his hands and feet, the men in the caves realize the Lord might actually work, and they come out.

You will never know how many men are in the holes around you until you go first. Then you'll be amazed at who comes running.

### Movement 4: First to the towel

[On screen: "He laid aside his outer garments, and taking a towel" John 13:4]

Now the last movement, because I've been talking about rivers and cliffs, and I need to show you what going first looks like in the kingdom of God specifically, or you'll walk out of here with the wrong picture.

Mark 10, right after Jesus was walking ahead of them toward Jerusalem. Two of his men come asking for the top spots. And Jesus says, verse 42, "You know that those who are considered rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them, and their great ones exercise authority over them. But it shall not be so among you." Then, verses 43 to 45: "But whoever would be great among you must be your servant, and whoever would be first among you must be slave of all. For even the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many."

Whoever would be first must be slave of all. In the kingdom, going first means going first into the low place.

John 13. It's the night before the cross. Verse 1 says Jesus "knew that his hour had come." Verse 3 says he knew "the Father had given all things into his hands." That's the most powerful man who has ever lived, on the most important night in history, fully aware of who he is. And verse 4: he "rose from supper. He laid aside his outer garments, and taking a towel, tied it around his waist. Then he poured water into a basin and began to wash the disciples' feet."

Nobody in that room was going to do that. It was the lowest job there was. Twelve men had walked in with filthy feet and every one of them looked at the basin and waited to see who would go first. And the Lord of all the earth went first. Including Judas's feet. And then verse 14: "If I then, your Lord and Teacher, have washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another's feet."

That's the model. Courageous leadership goes first to the towel. It goes first to the dishes nobody asked you to do. It goes first to the apology when you're only forty percent wrong. It goes first to the hard conversation with the friend everyone else has quietly given up on. It goes first to the tithe when money's tight. It goes first onto your knees in front of your kids: "Dad was wrong last night. I need to ask you to forgive me." Some of you think that would undermine your authority. It's the opposite. It's the most courageous thing your son will ever watch you do, and he will follow you anywhere after that.

And then, a few hours later, the garden. John 18:4. The soldiers come with torches and weapons, and Jesus, "knowing all that would happen to him, came forward." Came forward. First into the arrest. And verse 8: "If you seek me, let these men go." He went first and he covered the men behind him. Feet in the water and standing in the riverbed until they were all across.

Hebrews 12:2 gives him a title for this. "Looking to Jesus, the founder and perfecter of our faith." The word behind "founder" means the one who goes ahead. The pioneer. The trailblazer. Jesus is the first-goer of your faith. He went into death first so that every man who follows him can walk through it on dry ground.

[Beat.]

So here's what you need to hear. You are not the first one into any river you'll ever face. He already went. Into the temptation, into the grief, into the shame, into the grave. Your going first in your home and your church is just following the Man who's already standing in the riverbed with the ark, saying, "Come on. I've got it held. Cross."

## APPLICATION

[Shift posture. Practical register. On screen: Name it / Do this week / Tell a brother / Carry this]

Four moves. Same as every week.

### Name it

Name your river. Every man has one: the thing everyone around him is waiting on somebody to start, and the somebody is him. Finish this sentence with real words: "The river in front of me is _____."

The conversation with my wife about where we actually are. Praying out loud with my kids. Telling my small group the truth. Confronting my brother about Dad. Starting a men's table at church because nobody else has. Going to my son and owning what I did when he was eight.

Name it. Then name what you're afraid of, because that's why you're still on the bank.

### Do this week

Three things.

One. Feet in the water. Within seventy-two hours, one first move on the river you named. Not the whole crossing. The first step, where your feet get wet and nobody else's do. Say the first sentence. Pray the first prayer at the table tonight, out loud, badly. Send the text that opens the conversation. Do it before the water moves, because it won't move until you do.

Two. Stand in the riverbed. Pick one person you're responsible for leading: your wife, one of your kids, a young man at church. Ask them this week, to their face: "What do you need from me to get across what you're facing right now?" Then don't fix it in one conversation. Stand there. Check back in three days later. Leaders stay in the riverbed.

Three. First to the towel. One act of service in your own house this week that nobody asked you to do and nobody will thank you for. The thing your wife hates doing. The kid's bathroom. The bill nobody wants to open. Go first to the low place, quietly, and watch what it does to the room.

### Tell a brother

Tell one man your river and your seventy-two-hour step. Then say Jonathan's line to him: "It may be that the LORD will work." And ask him to be your armor-bearer this week. Or, if he's got a cliff of his own, be his. Say it out loud: "I'm with you heart and soul." Men follow men who go, and no man should climb alone.

### Carry this

One line.

"The water moves after the feet do."

Say it on the bank. Say it when you're waiting for a sign. Say it when the whole room is looking at the carpet. The water moves after the feet do.

## CLOSE

[Return to seated, direct to camera. Slow.]

Let me land this.

A river at flood stage. A handful of men with a word from God and wet feet. And a wall of water that did not stop until they stepped in, and did not start again until the last child was across.

A king with a crown and six hundred men, sitting. And his son climbing a cliff on his hands and feet with one man behind him, saying "it may be that the LORD will work," and an army of hiding men coming out of their holes because somebody finally went.

And the Lord of all the earth, on the night before he died, kneeling on the floor with a towel, going first into the lowest place in the room, and then walking forward into the torches saying "let these men go."

Brother, everybody in your house, everybody at your table, everybody in your church knows what needs to happen. They're waiting to see who goes first. God's answer to that question is you. Not because you're the bravest man in the room. Because you've been given a word, you've been given the presence of God to carry, and you've got a Pioneer who is already standing in the riverbed.

Same sentence every week. Say it with me.

A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward.

Next week we take the fourth mark, and I've been telling you for three weeks that it's the one the other three depend on. Expects the greater reward, God's reward. If you've ever wondered how a man keeps going first, keeps picking up the weight, keeps getting up off the mat, year after year, when nobody's clapping, next week is the answer.
