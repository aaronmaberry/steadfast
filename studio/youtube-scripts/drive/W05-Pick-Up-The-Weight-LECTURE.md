## OPEN

[COLD OPEN. No music under. Max seated at the table, direct to lens. Lower third: Max | Steadfast.]

Picture a man walking through his own house at eleven o'clock at night. Everybody's asleep. He's turning off lights. And as he walks, he passes things.

He passes the kitchen faucet that's been dripping since March. Somewhere in the back of his head: I'll get to it.

He passes his fourteen-year-old's door, closed, light still on under it, and he knows the kid's on his phone again, and he knows what's probably on that phone. Back of his head: his mom's better at that conversation.

He passes the stack of mail on the counter with the envelope from the credit card company that he hasn't opened. Back of his head: it's her spending, mostly.

He passes the bedroom where his wife fell asleep facing the wall, the way she has for about three months now. Back of his head: she's been in a mood.

He passes his own reflection in the dark window. The extra thirty pounds. The Bible on the nightstand he hasn't opened since Easter. Back of his head: it's been a hard season.

[Beat.]

Five weights. And as he walks past every one of them, without ever saying it out loud, he does the same thing. He checks whose name is on it. And every time, the answer comes back the same.

Not mine.

Not my fault. Not my job. Not my season. Not my department.

My name is Max. This is Steadfast. Twenty-four weeks, building men who don't move. This is week five.

[Lower third: Week 5 of 24 | Pick Up the Weight]

Last week we took the first mark, rejects passivity, and we watched Jesus tell a man who'd been waiting thirty-eight years to get up and walk. This week, the second mark. Here's the whole sentence. Say it with me.

A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward.

Accepts responsibility. Today I'm going to show you where the "not mine" reflex came from, and I'm going to show you a man who heard about a broken wall eight hundred miles away that he did not break, and picked it up anyway.

## CLAIM

[Straight to camera. Slow down.]

Here's the claim. Twice.

Responsibility is not the same thing as fault. A real man answers for what is his, not only for what he broke. And the weight was never meant to crush him, because he was never meant to carry it alone.

Again. Responsibility is not the same thing as fault. A real man answers for what is his, not only for what he broke. And the weight was never meant to crush him, because he was never meant to carry it alone.

Three parts to that claim. Let me make sure you have them.

Responsibility is not fault. Fault asks, who caused this? Responsibility asks, who answers for this? Those are different questions with different answers, and most men only know how to ask the first one. When a man only asks "whose fault," he will spend his life stepping around weights that have his name on them, because he can prove he didn't put them there.

A real man answers for what is his. In Genesis 2:15 God put the man in the garden "to work it and keep it." That's a field. Everything in the field is his to tend. When thorns come up in the field, the man doesn't launch an investigation into who planted them. He picks up a hoe.

And the weight was never meant to crush him. I have to say this part up front, because some of you hear "accept responsibility" and your shoulders drop, because you already feel like you're carrying everything and about to buckle. Hear me. God does not hand a man weight so he can watch him break. He hands a man weight so he can teach him to carry it the right way. And the right way is not alone.

## TEXT

[On screen: Genesis 3:9-12, then Nehemiah 1. Max opens Bible.]

Two texts today. The first one you've already seen. The second one is where we'll live.

Genesis 3, verse 9. God comes into the garden after the fall and calls to the man. "Where are you?" Verse 11, he asks him straight: "Have you eaten of the tree of which I commanded you not to eat?"

Verse 12. "The man said, 'The woman whom you gave to be with me, she gave me fruit of the tree, and I ate.'"

We looked at that two weeks ago. Hold it in your mind. The first man, handed the first weight, and his very first move is to check whose name is on it and read off everyone else's.

Now Nehemiah. Chapter 1.

[Read.]

"The words of Nehemiah the son of Hacaliah. Now it happened in the month of Chislev, in the twentieth year, as I was in Susa the citadel, that Hanani, one of my brothers, came with certain men from Judah. And I asked them concerning the Jews who escaped, who had survived the exile, and concerning Jerusalem. And they said to me, 'The remnant there in the province who had survived the exile is in great trouble and shame. The wall of Jerusalem is broken down, and its gates are destroyed by fire.'"

Verse 4. "As soon as I heard these words I sat down and wept and mourned for days, and I continued fasting and praying before the God of heaven."

Then he prays, and I want you to hear one line from the prayer. Verse 6: "confessing the sins of the people of Israel, which we have sinned against you. Even I and my father's house have sinned."

And then the last sentence of the chapter, verse 11, almost a footnote: "Now I was cupbearer to the king."

Chapter 2. It's now the month of Nisan. That's four months later. Nehemiah is serving the king wine, and the king notices his face. Verse 2: "Why is your face sad, seeing you are not sick? This is nothing but sadness of the heart." And Nehemiah writes, "Then I was very much afraid."

He tells the king about the city. Verse 4: "Then the king said to me, 'What are you requesting?' So I prayed to the God of heaven." And then he asks. Verse 5: "If it pleases the king, and if your servant has found favor in your sight, that you send me to Judah, to the city of my fathers' graves, that I may rebuild it."

Verse 8: "And the king granted me what I asked, for the good hand of my God was upon me."

Then he goes. Eight hundred miles. And in verse 17 he stands in front of the people who've been living next to that broken wall for a hundred and forty years and says, "You see the trouble we are in, how Jerusalem lies in ruins with its gates burned. Come, let us build the wall of Jerusalem, that we may no longer suffer derision." Verse 18: "And they said, 'Let us rise up and build.' So they strengthened their hands for the good work."

[Close the Bible. Look up.]

Here's what I need you to see before we go any further. Nehemiah did not break that wall. Babylon broke that wall, a hundred and forty years before he was born. He'd never seen it. He wasn't in Jerusalem. He had a job, a good one, in a palace in a different country. There is not one line of fault that runs from that broken wall to Nehemiah.

And he picked it up anyway.

Four movements. The first reflex. The weight that isn't your fault. The weight that is. And the weight you don't carry alone.

## MOVEMENTS

### Movement 1: The first reflex

[On screen: "The woman whom you gave to be with me" Genesis 3:12]

Go back to Adam for a minute, because you need to understand what you inherited.

God asks two questions in the garden. "Where are you?" And, "Have you eaten?" Look at what those questions are. The first is about location: are you at your post? The second is a yes-or-no about his own action. Neither one is "whose fault is this?" God is not running an investigation. He already knows. He's giving the man a chance to stand up and answer for what's his.

And the man answers with a chain of causes. The woman. Whom you gave. She gave me. And I ate. Three other parties named before he gets to himself, and when he gets to himself it's three words at the end of the sentence, said as quietly as possible.

That's the first reflex, and every man in this room has it. When a weight lands near you, before you think, before you pray, before you even look at it, your gut checks whose name is on it. And it's almost always looking for a reason the answer is "not mine."

Let me show you what the reflex sounds like now.

"She's the one who." Every man who's ever been married has started a sentence that way.

"My dad never." True, probably. And also, your dad's not raising your kids. You are.

"The church should." The church is people, and you're one of them, and the young men in your church who have no one discipling them are standing in your field.

"That's above my pay grade." Sometimes. But if you can see it, and you can fix it, and you're waiting for someone with a better title, that's Adam in a polo shirt.

"It's been a hard season." It has. And your body is still yours. Your soul is still yours.

[Beat.]

Now I want to show you how God thinks about this, because it's the opposite of the reflex. Ezekiel 22:30. God is looking at a nation in moral collapse, and here's what he says: "And I sought for a man among them who should build up the wall and stand in the breach before me for the land, that I should not destroy it, but I found none."

I sought for a man. Not, I sought for the man who was at fault. God wasn't looking for the guy who knocked the wall down. He was looking for any man who would stand in the gap. And the tragedy of the verse is, I found none. Every man in that nation could prove it wasn't his fault, and the wall stayed down.

Paul says it about the most basic weight there is. 1 Timothy 5:8: "But if anyone does not provide for his relatives, and especially for members of his household, he has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever." Provision is not a personality trait. It's an assigned weight. It's in the field. Nobody's asking whether you caused your family to need food.

So here's the question that replaces the reflex. When a weight lands near you, you don't ask "whose fault is this." You ask, "is this in my field?" If it is, you don't need any more information. It's yours.

### Movement 2: The weight that isn't your fault

[On screen: "Even I and my father's house have sinned." Nehemiah 1:6]

Now look at Nehemiah, because he is the exact opposite of Adam in every move.

Start with where he is. Susa. The citadel. He's the king's cupbearer, which means he's trusted, well paid, and close to power. He's got a paneled house, if you remember last week. Nothing about his life is broken. He's comfortable.

And his brother shows up from Judah, and Nehemiah asks a question. Verse 2, "I asked them concerning the Jews who escaped." Notice that. He asked. He didn't have to. A man who wants to keep his life simple doesn't ask about the wall eight hundred miles away. Nehemiah asked. Responsibility often starts with a man who's willing to know.

And they tell him. The wall's down. The gates are burned. The people are in trouble and shame.

Verse 4. "As soon as I heard these words I sat down and wept and mourned for days."

Men, look at that. He didn't hear about a problem and immediately start managing it. He didn't hear about it and shrug. He let it land on him. He sat down under it. He wept. For days. He fasted. He prayed. A weight that wasn't his fault became a weight he felt in his body.

That's the first thing responsibility does. It stops holding the problem at arm's length and lets it get heavy.

Then look at the prayer in verse 6. "Confessing the sins of the people of Israel, which we have sinned against you. Even I and my father's house have sinned." We. I. Nehemiah was born in exile. He didn't commit the sins that got Jerusalem burned. And he stands in front of God and says "we." He says "I." He puts himself inside the problem instead of standing outside of it with a clipboard.

Compare that to Adam. "The woman whom you gave." Adam puts everybody else inside the problem and stands outside it. Nehemiah puts himself inside a problem he could have honestly stayed outside of. That's the whole difference between a boy and a man in two sentences.

[Slow down.]

Now think about the weights in your life that aren't your fault.

Your wife carries wounds from before she ever met you. Somebody else did that. It's in your field.

Your son is struggling in a way that you can trace straight back to your own father, or to a culture you didn't design, or to a school you didn't pick. It's in your field.

Your church has thirty young men wandering around with no one showing them what a man is. You didn't build that church. You didn't drive those men's fathers away. It's in your field.

Your parents are getting old and they're not handling it well and your siblings have all found reasons to be busy. You didn't make anybody old. It's in your field.

The boy across the street whose dad left. You've never met the dad. That boy plays in your field.

Nehemiah's question was never "did I break this." It was "is this mine to weep over, pray over, and get up and go fix." And here's the part that'll surprise you. Chapter 1 is the month of Chislev. Chapter 2 is Nisan. Four months. He carried that weight in prayer for four months before he ever opened his mouth to the king. Responsibility isn't impulsive. He wept, he prayed, he waited on God for the part God hadn't told him yet, and he was ready when the door opened.

Then the door opened. The king said "what are you requesting," and Nehemiah writes the most honest sentence in the book: "Then I was very much afraid." And he prayed. And he asked anyway. Fear is not a sign you've picked up the wrong weight. It's usually a sign you've picked up the right one.

### Movement 3: The weight of your own

[On screen: "I have sinned against the LORD." 2 Samuel 12:13]

Now the harder weight. Not the one that isn't your fault. The one that is.

I want to show you two kings who both got caught, because they show you the two ways a man can respond when the weight has his own name on it.

The first is Saul. 1 Samuel 15. God gives him a clear command, Saul disobeys, and the prophet Samuel shows up. And Saul's first words, verse 13: "I have performed the commandment of the LORD." A lie. Samuel presses him. Verse 15: "They have brought them from the Amalekites, for the people spared the best." They. The people. Samuel presses again. Verse 21: "But the people took of the spoil." Finally, verse 24, Saul says "I have sinned," and then he can't help himself: "because I feared the people and obeyed their voice." He confesses and blames in the same breath. Adam's sentence, one thousand years later.

The second is David. 2 Samuel 12. David has done something far worse than Saul. Adultery, then murder to cover it. The prophet Nathan shows up with a story about a rich man who stole a poor man's only lamb, and David is furious at the rich man, and Nathan says, verse 7, "You are the man!"

And David says, verse 13: "I have sinned against the LORD."

That's it. That's the whole sentence. No "because." No "but." No "the woman." No "the pressures of leadership." Six words. He picks up the whole weight, all of it, with nothing left over for anybody else.

Then he goes home and writes Psalm 51. Verses 3 and 4: "For I know my transgressions, and my sin is ever before me. Against you, you only, have I sinned and done what is evil in your sight."

Two kings. Both sinned. One blamed and one owned. God took the kingdom from Saul. God called David a man after his own heart. Not because David's sin was smaller. It was bigger. Because David picked up the weight.

Proverbs 28:13 tells you why: "Whoever conceals his transgressions will not prosper, but he who confesses and forsakes them will obtain mercy." Concealing is what Adam did in the trees. Confessing is what David did in front of Nathan. And mercy runs to the one who confesses.

[Beat.]

So let me give you what an ownership statement actually sounds like, because most men have never heard one, and so they've never said one. Four parts.

One. Name exactly what you did. Not what happened. What you did. "I raised my voice at you in front of the kids." Not "things got heated."

Two. Name the harm without softening it. "That scared them, and it made you feel small in your own house."

Three. Ask. "Will you forgive me?" A question. Not "I'm sorry," which is a report on your feelings. A question that gives her the power.

Four. Say what changes. "When I feel that coming on, I'm going to leave the room and come back when I can talk like a man."

Now here's what an ownership statement never has in it. It never has "if." "If I hurt you." It never has "but." "I'm sorry, but you." It never has "you feel." "I'm sorry you feel that way." And it never has "mistakes were made," because there is no such thing as a weight with no name on it.

David: I have sinned against the LORD. That's the model. Say the short sentence.

### Movement 4: The weight you don't carry alone

[On screen: "Take my yoke upon you." Matthew 11:29]

Now, the last movement, and if you skip it, this whole lecture will hurt you instead of help you.

Some of you have been listening for twenty-five minutes and your chest is tight, because you're already carrying too much and I just added the drip in the kitchen, the credit card, your kid's phone, your wife's wounds, the young men at church, and your own sin. And you're thinking, I can't. I'm going to buckle.

Listen to Jesus. Matthew 11:28. "Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest."

Now watch the next verse, because it's not what you expect. He does not say, "and I'll take the weight off you." Verse 29: "Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me, for I am gentle and lowly in heart, and you will find rest for your souls. For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light."

Take my yoke. A yoke is not a device for taking weight off. A yoke is a device for pulling weight. But here's what you have to know about a yoke: it is built for two. A yoke is a wooden beam that joins two animals side by side, so that one load gets pulled by two necks. When Jesus says "take my yoke," he is saying: get under the beam with me. I'm on the other side. The weight is still there. But I'm pulling.

That's why the burden is light. Not because it's small. Because of who's under the other end.

Brother, the weight in your field is real. God is not going to take your wife out of your field, or your kids, or your body, or your sin. He is going to get under the yoke with you, and he is going to teach you how to pull. "Learn from me." That's a Man who carried the weight of the whole world to a cross, saying, I'll show you how this works.

And there's a second layer to this. Look at how Nehemiah finished. He didn't build the wall. Read chapter 3 sometime. It's a list of forty-some names, gate by gate, section by section, families building the part of the wall that was in front of their own house. Nehemiah picked up the weight, and then he said, verse 17, "Come, let us build." And verse 18: "So they strengthened their hands for the good work." He took responsibility for the whole wall and he carried it with everyone.

Paul puts both truths in the same paragraph. Galatians 6:5: "For each will have to bear his own load." Your field is yours. Nobody else answers for it. And three verses earlier, Galatians 6:2: "Bear one another's burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ." Your load is yours. The burdens that would crush you are for the brothers. Both are true. The man who won't let anyone under the yoke with him is not strong. He's proud, and he's going to drop it, and when he drops it, it lands on his family.

[Beat.]

Here's the order, then. Own it fully: "It's in my field, it's mine." Then say it out loud to God and pick up his yoke: "I can't pull this alone, and you said I don't have to." First Peter 5:7, "casting all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you." And then say it to a brother: "Come, let us build."

Full responsibility. Zero isolation. That's how a man picks up the weight without being crushed by it.

## APPLICATION

[Shift posture. Practical register. On screen: Name it / Do this week / Tell a brother / Carry this]

Four moves. Same as every week.

### Name it

Name two weights. Not five. Two.

The first is a Nehemiah weight. Something in your field that is not your fault and that you have been stepping around because it isn't. Your wife's old wound. Your kid's struggle. The young men at church. Your parents. The boy across the street. Name one.

The second is a David weight. Something that is your fault, that you have been carrying with a "because" attached. Name it in the short sentence. No "because." No "but."

Write both down. Then write "mine" next to each one.

### Do this week

Three things.

One. The Nehemiah move. For the weight that isn't your fault, sit down under it. Four days in a row, pray about it specifically, by name, before you do anything else about it. Let it get heavy. Then on day five, take one step. Have the conversation. Make the call. Ask the question you've been avoiding asking.

Two. The David move. One full ownership statement to one person this week. Four parts. What I did. The harm. Will you forgive me. What changes. No "if," no "but," no "you feel." Say it to their face, not in a text.

Three. Write your field. One page. At the top, your name. Underneath, everything that's yours to tend: your walk with God, your wife, each of your kids by name, your body, your money, your work, your word, your home, your church. Look at the whole field on one page. Then pray through it, line by line, and ask God to show you where you've been saying "not mine."

### Tell a brother

Tell one man both weights. Then do the thing most men never do: ask him to get under the yoke. Be specific. "Will you text me Thursday and ask if I had the conversation?" "Will you pray for my son every day this week?" "Will you come with me when I talk to my dad?" That's Galatians 6:2. You can't fulfill the law of Christ by yourself.

If you've been building with a brother since week two, you already know who to call.

### Carry this

One line for the week. Three parts.

"It's in my field. It's mine. I don't carry it alone."

Say it when you walk past the drip. Say it when you see the light under your kid's door. Say it when the "because" is on your tongue. It's in my field. It's mine. I don't carry it alone.

## CLOSE

[Return to seated, direct to camera. Slow.]

Let me land this.

The first man was handed the first weight and read off everyone else's name. And every man since has inherited that reflex. Not mine. Not my fault. Not my job. Not my season.

Then a cupbearer in a palace, comfortable and far away, heard about a wall he didn't break, and he sat down and wept. He said "we." He said "I." He carried it in prayer for four months, and then he asked a king for it with his knees shaking, and he went eight hundred miles, and he said "come, let us build," and a wall that had been down for a hundred and forty years went up in fifty-two days.

And a king who had done the worst thing a man can do stood in front of a prophet and said six words with no "because" in them. And God called him a man after his own heart.

Brother, you are going to walk through your house tonight. You are going to pass the weights. And you are going to feel the reflex. This time, don't check whose name is on it. Ask if it's in your field. And if it is, get under the yoke with Jesus, call a brother, and pick it up.

Same sentence every week. Say it with me.

A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward.

Next week, the third mark. It's called "Go First," and it's about a river that would not part until somebody's feet were in it. Subscribe and hit the bell so you don't miss it, because every week of this build stands on the one before it.

[Beat.]

Stand firm. Act like men. Be strong.

I'll see you next week.
