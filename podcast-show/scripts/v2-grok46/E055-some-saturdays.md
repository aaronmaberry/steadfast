## SEGMENT 1 · Which days

WES: Pull up a chair. Let's take the next few minutes on that supper table, because I want the whole room, not just the punch line.

JONATHON: It's VBS week at a church down the road. Ruby's in it, Theo's in the little kids' room. This year's big song has the word fire in it about forty times, plus hand motions over chicken like the table is a stage.

WES: Then she stopped and asked you the question you used as the hook.

JONATHON: She said, "Daddy, are you on fire for Jesus?" I said "Some days," because it was true and I was tired. She said, "Which days?" Dani put her fork down. I said, "Eat your chicken." That's my mom's line when she didn't have an answer and needed the room to move on.

WES: So you used your mother's dodge on a seven-year-old who asked you a real question.

JONATHON: I knew it while I was doing it. Later Dani said, "I wanted to hear your answer." I said I didn't have one. She said, "Mine's some days too." She's a nurse on nights. On fire at four in the morning holding somebody's baby, and flat at four in the afternoon trying to sleep through Theo. I thought she was the steady one.

WES: If you live with somebody who looks solid, they may be counting some days too, and they've been waiting for you to say it first.

JONATHON: Here's the part I'm not proud of. Thursday my VP said, "Great idea, Jonathon," in front of the whole company, and I felt it. Warm. Big. Same feeling I got in a church parking lot at twenty-nine. Wired to the same place in me. I can't always tell them apart.

[BOARD: A feeling is not a heading.]

WES: My quiet report fits. The truck, second week, noon every day. Rangers and the heat. Some days we don't say ten words. I've caught myself thinking this isn't doing anything.

JONATHON: Did anything change, or are you collecting silent lunches and calling it faithfulness because it sounds better than awkward?

WES: Wednesday he had two cold waters on the dash. He handed me one and didn't say anything. That's the whole change. I'll take a water.

WES: I keep thinking about Mr. Abernathy. Six years of Thursdays at a bad diner with a forty-year-old who mostly talked about himself. I never asked if he felt like it. I just assumed a man that faithful loved every morning. He probably loved some Thursdays. He came on all of them.

JONATHON: So the mark today is Work for God's Pay, because if you only work when you feel it, you're working for the feeling. That's a paycheck too. It's just not His.

WES: Look around your house this week. Find the thing you've been waiting to feel like doing. Then notice who already showed up without the feeling.

## SEGMENT 2 · The flat year

WES: Jonathon, you've been sitting on a year you haven't told. Tell it, and don't clean it up for the hand motions.

JONATHON: When I came to faith, at twenty-nine, it was big. I'd sit in the church parking lot after the service and cry. I read Matthew to Revelation in about two months. I went to a men's conference in Dallas and told Dani we were going to change everything. If Ruby had asked me then, I'd have said every day.

WES: And then it went flat, and you thought the first year had been a trick.

JONATHON: About a year in, Ruby was born, my numbers were down because I was home, and church felt like nothing. I'd pray and it was me talking to a blank ceiling. I thought I'd lost it. Maybe it was never real. Maybe it was just a high, like the leaderboard.

WES: What kept you in the room, because "I felt nothing" is usually how a man exits.

JONATHON: Dani kept getting up on Sunday, so I did. A man at that breakfast kept asking me back on Thursdays, and I went because I'd said I would. Somewhere in that year I noticed I was different. I wasn't drinking every Thursday anymore. Not stopped yet. I'd quit yelling at the TV. I was home for bath. I hadn't felt one thing while it was happening.

WES: So the days you felt it weren't the days it was working.

JONATHON: That's my answer to "Which days?" The flat year was when it took. I wanted the parking-lot tears to be the proof. The proof was a tired man who slowly became somebody his kids could live with.

[BOARD: 2 Corinthians 5:7 (NIV) · "For we live by faith, not by sight."]

WES: If you've got a flat year right now, hear that. The absence of the feeling is not the absence of God. It might be the year it takes.

JONATHON: I'll be real with you. I still want the parking lot back. I still check my chest on Sunday like I'm checking a battery. Most Sundays the battery looks fine and empty at the same time. I used to treat that as failure. I'm trying to treat it as ordinary.

---

## SEGMENT 3 · The fire decade

WES: We're about halfway, and I'll tell you mine, because I think I had the opposite of your flat year. I had a fire decade. And it wasn't what I thought it was.

JONATHON: That sounds like the dream version, the one guys ask for when they say they want to get serious.

WES: I came back to life at thirty-six, at Mr. Abernathy's table. For the next eight or ten years I was on fire. Three committees. A ball team. A class. The man they called to pray. Fifty-five hours a week and church three nights. People said, "Wes is on fire."

JONATHON: And then your wife asked you a question in the truck that the committees couldn't answer.

WES: I was about forty-five, coming home from a meeting, and she said, "Where are we going?" I said, "Home." She said, "No. You and me. Our family." I didn't have an answer. Ten years on fire, and I couldn't tell my wife where we were going.

JONATHON: That's wild, because from the outside you looked finished. From the inside you were a man collecting heat.

WES: A lot of that fire was for me. Some of it was God. A lot of it was a man who'd been asleep finally feeling useful, and he kept saying yes to anything that gave him the feeling. Supper at home on a Tuesday didn't feed it. So I was gone.

JONATHON: So now "on fire for Jesus" makes you nervous, not because the feeling is bad, but because you've seen what you'll do to keep it.

WES: I'll burn down my own house to feel warm. If Ruby had asked me then, I'd have said every day. And I was half gone.

[BOARD: Heat is not the same as light.]

JONATHON: Here's my problem, and I'm staying here a minute. If the feeling can lie, and the flat can be the real work, how does a guy know he's not just bored and calling it faithfulness? I can use your fire decade as an excuse to never care again.

WES: Faithfulness has fruit. The flat year made you home for bath. The fire decade made me late for supper. Look at the people under your roof. If the "fire" costs them, it isn't His pay. If the quiet year is making you truer, stay in it and stop asking it to entertain you.

## SEGMENT 4 · The Word

WES: I've got a little under ten minutes, and I want the Bible open, because Scripture is colder and better than we are. Second Corinthians five, verse fifteen.

JONATHON: Paul's talking about why he keeps going when it costs him. Not a conference. A reason that still works in a jail.

WES: "And he died for all, that those who live should no longer live for themselves but for him who died for them and was raised again."

[BOARD: Galatians 6:9 (NIV) · "Let us not become weary in doing good, for at the proper time we will reap a harvest if we do not give up."]

WES: Quick context. Paul is talking about the love of Christ controlling him. Look at what's not in that verse. No feeling. Facts. He died. He was raised. You live. And you don't live for yourself anymore. The fire's not in your chest. The fire's what He did. Same fact on a flat Tuesday as at the conference in Dallas.

JONATHON: "No longer live for themselves." That's the part that gets me. When I was on fire, I liked how I felt. This says the whole point is it stops being about me.

WES: If your destiny depends on how you feel about it, it's about your chest. The thing God has for you doesn't need your feelings' permission. You can do it bored, tired, in a truck talking about the bullpen. Paul kept going because of what was true, not because of what he felt.

JONATHON: So the feeling's not bad. I need you to say that, because somebody will hear this and throw away every good Sunday he ever had.

WES: The feeling's a gift. Ruby's hand motions are a gift. Don't make it the engine. It's the weather. You don't decide whether to go to work based on the weather.

JONATHON: "He died for all." Not for the men who feel it. For the man in the truck. For Virgil. For the kid who doesn't feel called. Same cross. That's why it doesn't move when the feeling moves.

WES: I've got a card in my wallet. Love God out loud, keep the people He gave me, and finish. Not one feeling on it. Love, keep, finish. Verbs. I think the man who wrote it had already been burned once.

## SEGMENT 5 · Some Saturdays

WES: Last few minutes on two Saturdays, because this has to leave the verse and get into a driveway.

JONATHON: Saturday was the crew again. I wanted another fire day, like the ramp and the lady at the mailbox. It was one rotten deck board. The owner's in the hospital. Her son called the church. Six of us, a hundred and one degrees, forty-five minutes. Nobody home. Nobody saw it.

WES: And you watched the man instead of the story.

JONATHON: I watched Virgil. He never says he runs it. He's got a pencil notebook in his truck. Address, fix, date. Pages of it. He checked the board, then the rails nobody asked about, then every screw on the storm door. Nobody was home to thank him.

WES: And you finally got his name, after two weeks of praying for a man you couldn't introduce.

JONATHON: Virgil. Seventy-seven. I told him I'd been praying for him, and he looked at me like I'd said something in another language. Then, "Well, I appreciate that," and back to loading. I asked how long. "Since my wife died." "When was that?" "Ninety-eight." Twenty-nine years. I asked if he likes it. He thought about it. "Some Saturdays." And he goes on all the other ones too.

[BOARD: Show up on the Saturdays that do not sing.]

WES: That's your answer to Ruby. Virgil's. Some Saturdays.

JONATHON: That's my answer. Not the hand motions. The notebook.

WES: I'll tell you where I'm doing this without the feeling. The barn. First Saturday I fed with my brother, I talked about it like a conversion. Two months later it's July at six, already hot, two men throwing hay. He says, "Well." I go home.

JONATHON: And Saturday you didn't want to go, which is the only version that counts for this conversation.

WES: Alarm at five-fifteen. Knee talking. I lay there doing the math on whether my brother would notice. He fed alone for thirty years. One Saturday won't matter.

JONATHON: Did you go, or did you win the math and stay in the sheets?

WES: I went. Pulled in at six-oh-two. Late, for us. He looked at his watch and said nothing. We fed. Nothing happened. That's most Saturdays for the rest of our lives, if I keep going. Two brothers throwing hay. I'm done making him feed alone.

## SEGMENT 6 · Pushback

WES: Jonathon, push on this, because a man listening is already building an exception.

JONATHON: Twenty-two. Everybody talks about finding your calling. He's graduating, he doesn't feel called to anything, and he's scared he'll miss it. If the feeling isn't the engine, does he just pick a random job and hope?

WES: You're not going to miss it. It's not hiding. It's already the next thing in front of you. A job you can do well. A church that needs a man on Wednesdays. A grandmother who needs a ride. Do the next thing for Him, then the next. Look back in ten years and you'll see a road.

JONATHON: I'll take the divorced dad. He thought his calling was his family. The shape changed. I'm staying partly unconvinced that "do the next thing" is enough for a man who lost the house.

WES: His kids are still his. Every-other-weekend is still fathering. Find one thing this month that has nothing to do with his situation. A crew. A kid at church. A widow's deck. It won't fix it. It'll remind him he's still for something.

JONATHON: Half-true. Some men need a counselor before they need a deck. If the flat has turned dark and stayed dark, tell somebody. A doctor, a pastor. If it scares you, call or text 988. Don't use Virgil as a way to skip help.

WES: Agreed on that. Virgil is not a substitute for a doctor. He's a picture of showing up after the feeling left.

JONATHON: Sixty-one. He used to feel God close every day. Last three years, nothing. He's afraid he lost it at his age. That's not a twenty-two problem.

WES: That's me in stretches. Feelings get quiet as men get older. That's not God leaving. Keep doing what you did when you felt it. Tell an older man. Don't carry the quiet alone.

JONATHON: One more, because I lived it. The only time I felt God was at a conference. Monday, nothing. I thought my faith might be fake. Don't dunk on that night. It was real.

WES: I'm not dunking on it. That night was real. The test is Tuesday. What did you do for somebody who will never be at a conference? Then enjoy the conference when it comes.

## SEGMENT 7 · What we're actually doing

WES: Here's what I'm actually doing. The porch prayer. Honest number was three nights out of fourteen. The other eleven I was tired, or the Rangers were on, or I just didn't. This week, every night. Out loud. Feel it or not. Friday I'll tell you how many I made. If it's four, I'll say four. I'm not going to round up on a conversation about not running on feelings.

JONATHON: Mine's the crew sheet through December. Last time I signed one Saturday. This week I'm putting my name on every one I can make, including the rotten-board days. And I'm going to tell Ruby about Virgil. A man who's fixed widows' porches every Saturday since before she was born. Some Saturdays he likes it. He goes on all of them. Then she can keep the hand motions, because she's seven, and the hand motions are still a gift.

WES: This week's challenge. Name one thing God has already put in front of you that you've been waiting to feel like doing. A person, a job, a prayer, a call. Then do it this week without the feeling. Notice afterward whether the feeling showed up. Either way, it counts.

JONATHON: If you're married, on a calm night, ask your wife, "Is there something you've seen me waiting to feel like doing?" Say your guess first. Don't argue with hers. Sit with it a day. If you're single, you need the next thing, not a title. There's a crew somewhere with a Virgil on it who'd love a younger back.

WES: If you're divorced, find one thing for someone else this month. If you're older and the feeling left, keep going to the table. Somebody younger is watching you show up without it, and that's the lesson he needs most.
