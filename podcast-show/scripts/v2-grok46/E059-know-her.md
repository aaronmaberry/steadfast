## SEGMENT 1 · The beach you invented

WES: Pull up a chair. Let's take the next few minutes on a porch Saturday, because I asked a question I thought I already knew.

JONATHON: "What's something about you I'd get wrong if somebody asked me?" That's a brave question for a man who has been married twenty-seven years and still packs the cooler like it's a ministry.

WES: She said, "You'd say I like the beach." Twenty-two summers. Same week in June. Same rental three times. She books it. She packs the cooler. She finds the place with the porch. Then she said, "I like that you like it."

JONATHON: Wait, she's been running your vacation so you can feel like a man who takes his family to the water? That's a report card, not a punch line.

WES: She likes mountains. Cool air in the morning. Somewhere she needs a jacket. She said so in 2004. Our daughter was two. I said the drive was too long. Fourteen hours. I closed it. I've been packing a cooler for a beach she never asked for.

JONATHON: So knowing her isn't remembering the Dairy Queen order from when you were twenty-four. Knowing her is asking again, because she's not a file you opened once.

[BOARD: An old answer can hide a new woman.]

WES: When I was twenty-four I knew her radio songs, her sister's birthday, her mother's maiden name, her Dairy Queen order. I courted her like a job. Then I got the job and stopped studying.

JONATHON: I did the same with Dani. Shifts, nurses, cilantro, the old R and B station. I used to call her on the drive home just to hear her turn the radio down. Then kids. Then I started giving her the number when she asked about a meeting, and never the part that made me feel small.

WES: Look around your house this week. What have you been getting wrong on purpose because the old answer is easier?

## SEGMENT 2 · The parking lot

WES: Your turn. Dani. And I want the H-E-B, not the cleaned-up version.

JONATHON: Thursday after the kids were down I asked, "What's something you've been doing lately that I don't know about?" She said, "I sing in the car." Since the Fourth, every drive home from the hospital. Scales, then the audition piece. She won't tell me which. She said I'd look it up and listen forty times, and she's right.

WES: She pulls into a parking lot to finish. H-E-B, or somewhere she can hide?

JONATHON: H-E-B. Usually twice through. Because if Ruby hears her, it's real, and if it's real and she doesn't get in, Ruby will know her mom tried something and didn't make it. She emailed the director herself. She has an August slot. She talked to her charge nurse about Tuesdays if she gets in. Then she said, "Don't come to the audition. Ask me how it went."

WES: So your job is to ask once a week and not drive by the store like a man running surveillance on his own wife.

JONATHON: That's my job. And here's the part I've got to say. When she said she wanted to sing, part of me heard that she was leaving. I know how that sounds. My dad left with a lamp. My chest does not always tell the truth. I heard leaving in a woman asking for Tuesday nights.

[BOARD: Fear will name a gift as a threat.]

WES: You're going to tell her that, or are you going to keep it as a confession she never hears?

JONATHON: I'm going to tell her that. Not as a speech. As a confession. "Part of me heard that you were leaving, and I'm sorry." Then I've got to be okay with Tuesdays out loud, not quiet. Quiet is how I make her carry my fear.

WES: We're about halfway, and I want you to hear this if your wife has a want that isn't about you. Her want isn't a verdict on your marriage. Your fear might be a verdict on an old story.

## SEGMENT 3 · The well

WES: My granddad had a hand-dug well. Stone sides, bucket, rope, crank. By my time there was a pump, but he kept the well. He used to say, "You can't yell at a well." You drop the bucket. You wait. You draw.

JONATHON: That's the picture for this, and I hate it, because I want her to just tell me. I want a faucet.

WES: Proverbs says the purposes of a person's heart are deep waters. You don't get deep water by yelling. You get it by drawing. That's a job. That's Go First. You go first with the question, then you wait with your mouth shut. I've been a man who asks and then fills the silence because silence makes me feel incompetent. Silence is part of the draw. Let it be silent.

JONATHON: I'll push on that. Some wives won't tell you because you have a history of fixing, or defending, or turning it into a joke. Drawing means you have to become safe again. That's on you, not on her depth.

WES: Agreed. I wasn't safe in 2004. I closed a fourteen-hour drive with a sentence. She never raised mountains again. That's not her being mysterious. That's me being loud. A well doesn't dry up because the water left. A well dries up because nobody comes with a bucket, or because somebody kicks dirt in the mouth of it and calls that settling.

JONATHON: I kicked dirt in with jokes. "You're fine. You're always fine." Dani isn't always fine. I said it so I could go back to email. That's not color. That's cowardice with a smile.

## SEGMENT 4 · The Word

WES: I've got a little under ten minutes, and I want the Bible open. Proverbs twenty, verse five. You want to read it?

JONATHON: "The purposes of a person’s heart are deep waters, but one who has insight draws them out."

[BOARD: Proverbs 18:13 (NIV) · "To answer before listening— that is folly and shame."]

WES: Quick context. Proverbs is wisdom for a man who thinks he already knows. Deep waters means you don't see the bottom from the porch. Insight isn't a personality. It's a practice. You draw. You don't assume.

JONATHON: "Draws them out." That's work. That's not a vibe and it's not a quiz. I asked one good question and found a whole parking lot I didn't know existed.

WES: First Peter says husbands live with their wives in an understanding way. That's the same job in a different book. Understand is a verb you keep doing. I understood her at twenty-four and then I retired.

[BOARD: 1 Peter 3:7 (NIV) · "Husbands, in the same way be considerate as you live with your wives, and treat them with respect as the weaker partner and as heirs with you of the gracious gift of life, so that nothing will hinder your prayers."]

JONATHON: That verse is going to make somebody mad about "weaker partner," and I get it. The end of the sentence is the one that scares me. Heirs. Same gift. If I don't know her, my prayers hit a ceiling I built.

WES: If your prayers feel like a blank ceiling, start with the woman in the other chair. You may have been answering before listening for years.

## SEGMENT 5 · What else

WES: Last few minutes on what I'm actually doing. I'm going back to the porch. "What else have you told me that I didn't hear?" I'm going to write what she says on a card for my wallet. I'm not booking mountains yet. I'm listening first, because I booked a beach for twenty-two years. What about you?

JONATHON: I'm telling Dani the leaving sentence. Then I'm asking once a week about the song, and I won't drive by the H-E-B. Courting her I knew the radio. Knowing her now means I don't steal the piece.

WES: Pushback. A man says, I asked and she said she's fine. Now what?

JONATHON: Fine is sometimes a locked well. Ask a smaller question. "What's something you've been doing lately that I don't know about?" Then sit there. If she tests you with a small one, don't yawn. The next one is deeper.

WES: A man says, I know her. We've been married thirty years.

JONATHON: You knew her. Then she changed, and you didn't update the file. Thirty years is a reason to ask more, not less.

WES: A man says, we don't talk like that.

JONATHON: Then you go first. You tell her one true thing she doesn't know about your week. Not a dump. One true thing. Then ask. Drawing starts with you putting a bucket in.

## SEGMENT 6 · The challenge

WES: This week's challenge. Ask her one question you don't know the answer to. Write her answer down. Don't argue. Don't fix. Sit with it a day. Then ask one more, smaller, if she lets you.

JONATHON: If you're dating, same job. If you're single, ask an older married man what he got wrong about his wife and stayed wrong for years. If you're divorced, you can still learn the job for the next conversation with your kids' mom, without pretending it will turn back into a romance.

WES: Look around your house this week. The woman in it's not the woman you married in every way, and that's not a failure. That's a well. Draw. I packed a cooler for a beach she never asked for, and I called that love because I was in the picture.

## SEGMENT 7 · Two more rooms

WES: I want two more rooms before we go, because a man will hear the beach story and think his house is fine. First room: the meeting. She asks how it went. You give her the number. You don't give her the part that made you feel small. I've done that for years. I thought I was protecting her. I was protecting my pride.

JONATHON: I give Dani the number. "It was fine. We moved two counties." I don't tell her the VP made me feel twelve. Then I wonder why she doesn't know me. She can't draw water I won't put in the well.

WES: Second room: the trip. I've been the man who books what he likes and calls it "for the family." Rockport was for me. The porch was for me. She made it gentle so I could keep being the man who takes them to the water. That's not partnership. That's a performance she has been producing. You hear that, or are you still calling Tuesdays a hobby she can drop?

JONATHON: Dani produces Tuesdays for me. Sleep, kids, the house, then work. If I don't ask what the singing costs her, I will treat the audition like a hobby she can drop when the baby is sick. It's not a hobby. It's a woman coming back to life.

WES: So knowing her includes knowing what it costs. Not so you can talk her out of it. So you can carry the cost with her. I'm not booking mountains this week. I'm asking what else I didn't hear. That's the bucket. That's all I've got.

JONATHON: And if she says the real one and you freeze, don't recover with a joke. Write it down in front of her. "I'm writing this so I don't lose it." That's insight. That's not a quiz.

WES: A man at my table said his wife told him she wanted to finish a degree when the youngest started kindergarten. He said, "What for?" He meant money. She never raised it again. He told us that like it was a curiosity. It was a well he kicked dirt into.

JONATHON: That's the one that sits with me, because "What for?" is my voice. I've asked Dani "What for?" about rest, about singing, about seeing my mom without a job attached. I'm trying to retire that sentence.

WES: Retire it. Replace it with "Tell me more." Then shut up long enough that she believes you. I've about twenty-seven years of practice at the other thing. I'm late. I'm still going to the porch.

JONATHON: I'll be real with you. I wanted this to be a technique. Three questions. A walk. A script. The technique is useless if your face says you already know. The walk is useless if you spend it solving. The script is useless if you argue with the answer.

WES: Then throw the technique out and keep the job. Ask. Write. Sit. Draw. If you need a walk, walk. If you need a porch, porch. The place isn't the point. The bucket is the point.

JONATHON: Single men, this still belongs to you. You're practicing on friends and sisters and the woman you're not marrying yet. Don't treat a first date like a deposition. Treat a person like deep water. Ask one thing you don't know. Then listen like it matters.

WES: Divorced men, you can still repent of "What for?" without rewriting the past into a romance. You can still learn to draw with your kids. You can still become the man who does not answer before he listens. That's not getting her back. That's getting you back.

JONATHON: Older men, if you've been married forty years and you think you know, ask anyway. She has a Tuesday you have never seen. I watched Dani's dad talk golf for a decade and then one Sunday he talked about his brother. The water was there the whole time. Somebody finally dropped a bucket.

WES: I've got about a minute and I want it on you. Pick the question. Not a cute one. A question whose answer might change your week. Then ask it on a calm night, not at the end of a fight. Then write what she says. Then don't fix it before Monday.

JONATHON: And if she cries, don't panic. Deep water sometimes comes out the eyes. Sit there. Hand her a towel. Don't explain her to herself. That's yelling at the well.

## SEGMENT 8 · The walk and the sink

WES: One more picture, because I don't want you leaving with only a question. My wife and I used to walk to the Dairy Queen every Friday night, about a mile each way from her mother's house. I knew her daddy's bird dogs, her sister's boyfriend, the name she wanted for a daughter. I asked questions because I wanted her. Then I got her, and I stopped wanting the answers. That's the sin under this. Not ignorance. Retirement. I retired from the job that got me the woman, and I called the retirement peace.

JONATHON: I called Dani on her drive home to hear the radio go down. That was knowing her. Then I started sending "K." For about nine years. She told me later she wanted a call sometimes. I thought "K" was efficient. It was me leaving the well and calling it work.

WES: So if your wife has been getting "K" from you, that's the assignment. Call. Ask. Write it down. I'm not above this. I packed a beach she does not want and I text "K" like it's a personality.

JONATHON: And if she doesn't want a walk, don't make the walk the test. Some women talk in a car. Some talk in the dark. Some talk while they dry a pan. Find the room where she actually speaks, then shut up in that room.

WES: I've been the man who asks in the wrong room and then says she never talks. That's on me. The porch is her room. The truck is sometimes mine. I need to go to her room.

JONATHON: Dani's room is the sink after lasagna. That's where she told me I never asked how she was after Theo. Not how the baby was. How she was. I had been asking the wrong well for a year.

WES: Then that's your map. The sink. The porch. The parking lot. The walk. Go there. Drop the bucket. Wait. Draw. Don't yell. Don't "What for?" Don't book the beach again unless she asks for the beach. What are you actually doing this week?

JONATHON: Wait, back up. Are you asking what else you didn't hear, or booking mountains so you can feel like you listened?

WES: Asking. Card in the wallet. Sit with it a day. I'm not booking yet. I booked a beach for twenty-two years.

JONATHON: Can I ask you something hard?

WES: Go.

JONATHON: If she names a degree, are you going to say "What for?" with nicer words, or "Tell me more" and mean it even if your chest wants a number?

WES: [beat] Tell me more. I mean it. I've yawned at the little ones. I called them preferences. They were her telling me how to live with her. The little ones are the training for the real one. If you skip them, you have not earned the mountains. What are you picking, for real?

JONATHON: The leaving sentence. Then the song once a week. I won't drive by the store. If I fail, I'll say I failed, not that she got quiet. And I will call her instead of sending a letter K at least once, because nine years of that's long enough.

WES: That's fair, but— don't stack three so you can miss two. Pick one.

JONATHON: [laughs] Leaving sentence. That's the one. No, hold on. You've got a habit of packing a cooler and calling it love. Are you actually going to her porch, or asking in the truck because the truck is yours?

WES: [laughs] Porch. Her room. The truck is sometimes mine. I need to go to her room. TV off at supper isn't cute. A day's notice before company isn't cute. A phone call instead of a letter K isn't cute. Those are the little wells. If you yawn at those, she won't hand you the real one.

JONATHON: Then I say the leaving sentence at a sink that already has a pan. Does that sound like a quota?

WES: It sounds like a man who knows the room. Ask in the room where she speaks, or you never will. That's the whole takeaway. Knowing her is a job. Memory isn't the work.
