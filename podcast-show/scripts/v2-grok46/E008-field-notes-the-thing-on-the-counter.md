## SEGMENT 1 · Check the box

WES: Let's take the next few minutes on the counter, because Friday will show if it moved. I already used the funny letter. You get the rest of me first, then you go.

JONATHON: You get the letter, and then you owe the twenty minutes, because "K" isn't the story. The story is what happened after you sat in the truck.

WES: Wednesday, six fifty-nine. I was in the truck out by the barn, because I didn't want an audience. I called a minute early so I wouldn't lose my nerve. He picked up, and the first word out of his mouth was "Well."

JONATHON: Hold on. He answers the phone with "Well"? That's a whole personality in one syllable, and now I understand your family a lot better.

WES: It runs in the family. My dad said it. He says it. I say it. I told him I'd been meaning to call since Thanksgiving. He said, "I know." Then I said the line I'd been carrying two months. "What you said at Mom's was mostly right."

JONATHON: "Mostly right." Yeah, and— that's the kind of sentence that can either open a door or slam one, and I've sat through both versions at work. And then what?

WES: Nothing, for a while. Long enough I checked the phone to see if the call dropped. Then he said, "I didn't need you to say I was right. I need you at Mom's doctor appointment on the ninth."

JONATHON: So he didn't want the apology speech. He wanted a man in the room. That's a different ask, and it's heavier, because now you have to show up with your body, not just your voice.

WES: I'm going. February ninth. I told my job. And I want this on the record, because one call does not fix two months. He was still a little cold. We talked twenty minutes, mostly Mom's hip and his cattle. It's not finished. It's started.

[BOARD: Started is a door. Finished can wait.]

JONATHON: Twenty minutes is a filibuster in your family. I'll take started. Started is more than a cease-fire, and a cease-fire is what you called it when you left early to beat traffic that doesn't exist.

WES: Then I asked my wife the question. "Is there something you've been waiting on me for?" I braced for a marriage speech. She said, "Your knees."

JONATHON: Your knees. You've been limping since October, and you keep calling it age, which is how a man talks himself out of a doctor until somebody who loves him uses two words.

WES: She's asked five times. I keep saying I'll get to it. So I called Thursday morning. Appointment is February third. Then she said the line I didn't expect. "I'm not asking because of your knees. I'm asking because I want you around when we're old."

JONATHON: That's twenty years hiding inside a limp. A lot of men listening are waiting on a doctor and calling it toughness. Make the appointment. Then tell one man you did it.

## SEGMENT 2 · The other boxes

WES: Box checked on my side. Both of them. Your boxes now, and I want Thursday's review first, not the polished one.

JONATHON: Thursday I sat down with the guy I'd moved three times. I told him the numbers, what has to change, and what I'll do to help. I was braced for him to be mad. He sat there, and then he said, "Thank you. I knew. I've been waiting for somebody to say it."

WES: He was waiting too. Your delay was keeping him stuck, and you were calling it kindness.

JONATHON: I thought I was being nice. I was being a cease-fire in a button-down. He already knew he was underwater. Nobody would say it, so he could not fix it. Waiting isn't mercy when the other man is standing in the dark.

WES: And Dani. The October fight you never finished.

JONATHON: I said, "We never finished that. Can we pick a night?" She looked at me like I'd been replaced by a different man, the suspicious version. Then she said yes. Sunday, February seventh, after the kids are down. It's on the kitchen calendar, next to the big circle for Pancake in August.

WES: A little circle and a big circle. That's patience with a date.

JONATHON: Then I asked her the question. She said, "Since I redid the kids' rooms at Christmas, you text me at bedtime every shift asking where things are. I've been waiting for you to learn the new rooms." Theo's pajamas are in a bin now. I found the bin Wednesday. Zero texts.

WES: You learned the house you already live in. That's a small box, and it's still a box.

JONATHON: And the single check came by text, because that's how my nephew talks to me. He asked his roommate what he'd been putting off. The roommate didn't look up. He said, "The dentist. You've been chewing on the left side since Halloween." My nephew sent me the confirmation email. That's how I know it happened. I wrote back "proud of you" and he sent a thumbs-up, which is still more than "K."

[BOARD: A confirmation email beats a promise.]

WES: A picture of a confirmation is better than "I'll get to it." I'll take that from a twenty-two-year-old.

## SEGMENT 3 · The Word

WES: We're about halfway, and I want the Bible open for one sentence, because the honest question this week isn't "how do I try harder." It's "how do I stop waiting when I'm genuinely tired." Jonathon, Matthew eleven, verse twenty-eight.

JONATHON: "Come to me, all you who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest."

[BOARD: Matthew 11:30 (NIV) · "For my yoke is easy and my burden is light."]

WES: Jesus is talking to tired people, not to people who finished their list. He doesn't say come once you've cleaned it up. He says come weary. The first move isn't the clicking fan in the youngest's room. It's go to God tired and tell him you're worn out and there's still a thing sitting on the counter.

JONATHON: I'll be real with you, that's the prayer I actually have most nights. I'm worn out, Dani's on nights, the kids are still awake in my head, and there's a thing I already know I should do. I've been treating tired like a hall pass.

WES: Tired is real. It's not a character flaw. There are two kinds, and they feel the same at seven in the evening. One is the body that needs to stop. The other is avoiding something and calling it tired.

JONATHON: How do you tell them apart when both of them feel like a couch and a dark kitchen?

WES: Here's my test. If somebody called and said there was free brisket at the end of the street, would you get off the couch?

JONATHON: Yes. Immediately. I would put on shoes I could not find in the light. So I guess a lot of my tired is the avoiding kind, which is annoying to admit out loud.

WES: Then you're not that tired. You're avoiding. If you wouldn't get up for free brisket, you're actually tired, and rest isn't a sin. Go to bed. Tomorrow you will have ten minutes.

[BOARD: Psalm 127:2 (NIV) · "In vain you rise early and stay up late, toiling for food to eat— for he grants sleep to those he loves."]

## SEGMENT 4 · From the table

WES: Look at the thing still sitting on the counter. The question men keep sending is the two-tired-people version. Dani coming off three nights. You with the kids and a full day. A pile of dishes. Who goes first?

JONATHON: That's actually my house. We stand in the kitchen measuring who had the worse day, and the dishes win every time. Here's my problem with "you go first." What if I'm more tired than she is? Can I say that without sounding like a child?

WES: You can say it. You just can't use it as the reason the pile stays there. Tired is information. It's not a verdict that the other person has to move.

WES: Can you actually measure it? Honestly? Every marriage I've sat with, both people think they're the tired one. So stop measuring and move. When you move first, she usually gets up too. When you both wait, nobody gets up.

JONATHON: And if she really is more tired? Twelve-hour shift, three deliveries, she can barely stand in her own kitchen?

WES: Then you send her to bed and you do it, and you don't mention it in the morning. Mentioning it's collecting pay. Let God see it.

JONATHON: I would mention it. I know I would. I want credit the way I want a closed deal. That's the part I have to kill on purpose.

WES: Kill it. Then do ten minutes. That's the tool, and I brought the chicken.

---

## SEGMENT 5 · The ten-minute start

WES: Last few minutes on the thing on the counter, before it becomes furniture. This is my wife's ceramic chicken. She's had it since 1999. It's the most reliable thing in our house.

JONATHON: You brought a chicken to the table and you want me to take this seriously. Fine. Tell me the rule before I turn this into a joke I can't take back.

WES: Pick the one thing you've been waiting on. Set a timer for ten minutes. Not an hour. Ten. Phone in the basket. For ten minutes you do that thing and nothing else. When it dings, you're allowed to stop. No guilt.

JONATHON: And you don't stop, because once you're in it the hard part is already behind you. I need you to say whether I have to mean the stop, or whether this is a trick my brain will see coming.

WES: You have to mean it. Your brain is very smart about getting out of things. If it's a phone call, the ten minutes is getting ready. Sit in the truck, write the two lines, and when the chicken dings, you dial.

JONATHON: My version is the watch. Ruby's watch beeps every hour, and I never figured out how to turn it off. When it beeps at seven, I start the thing. Bath, dishes, the email I've been walking around.

WES: So the watch finally has a job. If you fail the brisket test, skip the chicken and go to bed. Rest is how God made you. Tomorrow the ten minutes will still be there. I sat in the truck ten minutes before I dialed. That ten minutes was the start. The call was what came after.

[BOARD: Ten minutes, then permission to quit.]

JONATHON: And if the thing is bigger than ten minutes, like a will, or a brother, or a doctor, the ten minutes is still the start. You don't have to finish the whole weight tonight.

WES: You don't have to finish it. You have to start it. What's the ten minutes tonight? Not the whole will. The first ten.

JONATHON: The lawyer email. I already wrote it in my head. That's not started. Started is hitting send.
