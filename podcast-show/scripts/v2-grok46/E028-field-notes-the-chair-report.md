## SEGMENT 1 · Check the box

WES: Let's take the next few minutes on the receipt, because I promised a man and a finish line. Tuesday night I called my oldest. Twenty-one. The apprentice. I wanted the shoe detail to go to somebody who had known my dad, and he's the oldest grandkid.
JONATHON: He never really knew your dad, and he's the oldest grandkid. Yeah, and—he was eleven when Dad died. What'd you tell him?
WES: He was eleven when Dad died. I told him Saturday nights, the farm section, the tin of polish, me handing Grandpa the brush. Not saying anything. He was quiet a while. Then he said, "You polish yours on Saturday nights." I said, "Yeah. I guess I do." He said, "I used to hand you the brush."

JONATHON: That's not a cute line. That's a boy copying you while you thought you were alone at a table.

WES: I don't remember that. At all. He does. He said he was about six. He would sit across from me at the kitchen table and hand me the brush. I asked him, "Did I say anything to you?" He said, "Not really. You were watching the game on the little TV." That was the fog years.
JONATHON: [beat] He was watching the little TV. That was the fog years. Stay there. He would have been six in 2012.
WES: He would have been six in 2012. I don't remember. My dad didn't say anything to me across that table. Then I sat at my own table and didn't say anything to my son. I thought the shoes were mine. They were my dad's, and then they were his.

JONATHON: What did you say after he handed you that, because I would have joked to get out of the quiet.

WES: I said, "I'm sorry I didn't talk to you." He said, "It's okay. I liked it." Same sentence I used about my dad on Tuesday. I loved it, and there wasn't anything in it. Two generations. I asked him to come over Saturday night. He said, "I'm not driving twenty minutes to watch you polish shoes." Then he said, "What time?" Seven. He's coming. I'm going to talk this time.

[BOARD: Ask memory before you lecture.]

WES: I told him one more thing on that same call. My dad sat top row, alone, at every one of that boy's games and never said a word after. When we cleaned out his truck after he died, the glove box held a rubber-banded stack of programs from every game. When my oldest was fourteen he asked, "Did Grandpa like me?" I said, "Of course he did, buddy." A true summary.
JONATHON: Yeah. Of course he did, buddy. A true summary. What'd you do after that, because Tuesday you finally gave him the programs.
WES: I had the programs and I didn't give them. Tuesday I gave them. He was quiet a long time. Then he said, "I wish I'd known that at fourteen." So do I. The summary was true. The stack was what he needed.

JONATHON: A boy asked you a real question at fourteen and you gave him a soft true. Soft true is still a hide when you have a glove box full of paper.

WES: The finish line. The paper is back on the fridge. I put it back before he got home Tuesday. Tomorrow is Saturday ten. Two after that, the seventeenth and the twenty-fourth. I haven't said one word about insurance. Sunday is the pot roast. My brother and I ask Mom what she wants, together, after lunch.
JONATHON: You and your brother ask Mom what she wants, together, after lunch. Hold on. Then you both shut up. That's the hard part.
WES: Then we both shut up. We practiced shutting up on the phone Wednesday. He's worse at it than I am. The recliner is still in his downstairs room. He says if Mom does not want it, it's his. He has been sitting in it. I think he has been sitting down there thinking about her.

[BOARD: Leave the signed paper alone.]

JONATHON: Mine went to a man I didn't plan. Thursday at practice the twin's dad asked about the lamp. I told him the short version. He picked up a ball, stood there, and said, "Mine took the TV." Then, "We didn't have a TV for three years. My mom said we didn't need one." He threw the ball in the bucket and went to help his daughter with her glove. Two guys on a field. About forty seconds. That's the most I've talked to a man about my dad who wasn't you.

WES: Forty seconds is a whole conversation if both men stay in it. You didn't dress it up. That's a check.

## SEGMENT 2 · One honest sentence

WES: We're about halfway, and I want Ruby now, because she's the tool this week. One honest sentence. How did you build it, because I know you wrote it twice and crossed both out.

JONATHON: Monday morning, first draft. "Grandpa lives far away and we don't see him." Crossed out. She already had far away. Dani gave her that in the car a month ago. She didn't ask where he lives. She asked if I've got one. Second draft was three paragraphs. A whole speech. Crossed out. That one was for me. That was me trying to fix twenty-eight years in a bedtime.

WES: And then you asked Dani. I want the part you still don't agree on, because unsettled is the report.

JONATHON: She said, "She's seven. Far away is enough. You don't need to say left." I said she already has far away. She asked if I've got one. Dani said left is a big word for seven. I said it's the true word. Nobody won. We didn't settle it. She still thinks left was too much. I still think she's wrong. Respectfully. She's a nurse. She tells families hard things for a living. I'm telling it with her permission. She does not concede.

WES: Wait, back up. You still don't agree, and you said left anyway. Did you tell her you were going to use the word?

JONATHON: I told her. She does not concede. I said it anyway. That's the report. Unsettled, and said.

[BOARD: Size the truth to the child.]

WES: So what did you actually say, in the room, with the lights off, because the card isn't the girl.

JONATHON: Tuesday. Bedtime. Lights off. Ruby in bed. Me on the floor. "I do have a dad. He lives in Louisiana. He left when I was nine. It was sad, it wasn't my fault, and I'm not going anywhere." Four true things and one promise. Then I stopped. Stopping was the hardest part. It was quiet. Then from the bed, "Does he know about me?"

WES: That's not a question I would have written down either.

JONATHON: I had a true answer. "He saw your picture. Easter. In the bluebonnets." She said, "Did he like it?" I said, "He did." For one second I was angry at him. Not about the lamp. About her. She hides Easter eggs for her brother. He has had seven years and a phone. Then the second passed, because she was waiting. Two weeks ago that heart was the saddest thing on my phone. Tuesday night it was the only true good thing I had to tell her. Still no message. I checked anyway.

WES: Did she say anything else after the like?

JONATHON: She said, "I'd be sad if you left." I said, "I'm not leaving." She said, "I know. I'm just saying." Then she rolled over. I sat on the floor for a while. I wanted a glow. I got a girl who already knew I was staying and still needed to say she would be sad.

WES: I know. I'm just saying is a child checking the chair in her own room. She wasn't asking for a speech. You stayed. That's the inch.

## SEGMENT 3 · The Word

WES: We've got a little under eight minutes, and I want the verse, because the question this week is how you talk to your kids about your dad. Paul is talking about mouths, not fathers, and that's the right door.

JONATHON: Ephesians four, twenty-nine. I read it on the floor after she rolled over. "Do not let any unwholesome talk come out of your mouths, but only what is helpful for building others up according to their needs, that it may benefit those who listen." According to their needs. Not my need to explain. Hers. She needed to know it was true, it wasn't scary, and I'm not leaving. That's what built her up. So which is it? My need to explain twenty-eight years, or her need to know she's safe?

[BOARD: Psalm 34:18 (NIV) · "The Lord is close to the brokenhearted and saves those who are crushed in spirit."]

WES: Hers. Unwholesome is two things in a kitchen. The trash talk about your dad. Kids hear your grandfather is a deadbeat and they hear something about half of themselves. And the other unwholesome. The fake-nice. Grandpa is just very busy. That's a lie in a clean shirt. Kids figure those out about age twelve. I gave my oldest a clean shirt at fourteen. Of course he did, buddy. True. Soft. Not the stack. Tuesday I gave him the stack. I can't get fourteen back. I can still talk at seven on Saturday.

JONATHON: A lie in a clean shirt is what I almost wrote on the first card. Far away and we don't see him. She already had the map. She asked if I've got a dad. My dad still has not written. A like is still the last thing. I used the like because it was true. I didn't dress it into a reunion. I wanted a paragraph from Louisiana. I got a heart and a girl who said she would be sad.

## SEGMENT 4 · From the table

WES: Last few minutes on the rules, from two men who have now done it once each, badly in spots. One, it's true. Every word. Kids can smell a soft lie. Two, it's short. If it does not fit on an index card, it's a speech. Three, it ends with what is safe now. For Ruby that was I'm not going anywhere. Four, then you stop, and you let them ask the next one. Five, if they ask one you can't answer, I don't know is an honest sentence too.

JONATHON: If your kids are older they can hold more. They might need your part too. Grown kids might already know more than you think. Wes's boy handed him a brush he had forgotten. Ask them what they remember before you tell them anything. And if you're divorced, the sentence is about your dad, not their mom.

WES: And if your dad was dangerous, the sentence can be, my dad wasn't safe, and that's why you have not met him. You're safe with me. That's true. That's enough for a child. Give the true short line. Then stay for the next question. Stay is the part I failed at fourteen. Stay is the part I'm trying tomorrow night, with newspaper, with a boy who is coming even though he said he wouldn't.

JONATHON: My sister on the lamp. I told her I wanted to replace it. She said, "Don't buy her a lamp. She'll think you're making a point. Take her to pick one out." Mom's birthday is May eighteenth. The plan is that weekend. I will walk a store. I didn't tell Ruby a lamp story. She got a sentence. The lamp is my anger. The sentence is her need. Those aren't the same job. Can I ask you something hard? Are you actually going to talk at seven on Saturday, or is the little TV going to win again?

WES: I'll talk. [laughs] He said he wasn't driving twenty minutes to watch me polish shoes, then he asked what time. That's a boy who wants a chair with words in it. I won't hand him a mute.

[BOARD: Take her to choose the light.]

WES: Tuesday we sit with a dad who is still alive. Twice-a-year phone calls. What you owe him. What you don't. I will report on the shoes after I've sat with my son and actually talked. Until then: write the sentence. Say it. Stop. Let them ask. If you live alone, write it to a brother, or to God, and then send one true short line to a person you have gone quiet on.

JONATHON: I still want a message. I still check. I still think left was the right word. Dani still thinks it was big. We're both still here. The girl is asleep in a house where I said I'm not going anywhere. I named the empty chair. I didn't heal it. Then I stopped.
