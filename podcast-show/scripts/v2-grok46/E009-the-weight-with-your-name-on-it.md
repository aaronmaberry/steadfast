## SEGMENT 1 · The check-engine light

WES: Let's take the next few minutes on the light you've stopped seeing. You first. The will.

JONATHON: Dani and I've been saying "we should do a will" for three years, since Theo was born. Every time one of us gets on a plane, somebody says it. Then we land, and nobody says it again until the next plane. We don't take that many planes. We've got two kids. We don't go anywhere.

WES: So it stays a sentence you say in the air and forget on the ground.

JONATHON: Christmas, at Dani's parents' place in New Braunfels. Her mom is at the table, and out of nowhere, over pie, she says, "So if something happens to you two, who gets the kids?" The whole table goes quiet. Dani looks at me. I look at my pie. I said, "We're working on it."

WES: Were you working on it?

JONATHON: We weren't working on it. We weren't anywhere near working on it. On the drive home Dani said, "That's four times I've brought it up." I said, "Has it been four?" She said, "Five, counting tonight." She wasn't mad. She said it like the weather.

WES: Why haven't you done it? It's not hard. You call somebody. You fill out forms.

JONATHON: That's what I told myself. Paperwork. I hate paperwork. That's not it. The forms aren't the hard part. The hard part is one line. Who raises Ruby and Theo if we're both gone. That's either my sister in Pasadena, who I love, who has never once kept a plant alive, or Dani's parents, who are seventy-two and seventy-four. To answer that I have to sit in my kitchen and imagine I'm dead. And pick.

WES: [quietly] Yeah.

JONATHON: So I didn't. For three years. You know what it's like? It's the check-engine light. It comes on, you're worried, a week goes by, the car still runs, and pretty soon you don't even see it. That's the will. Glowing on our dashboard since Theo was born, and I drive around it every day.

[BOARD: A light you drove around until it felt normal.]

WES: And whose light is it?

JONATHON: I always thought it was both of ours, which in my house means it's Dani's, because she does the paperwork. I sat with it this week. It's mine. I'm the one who says out loud I want to protect this family. I'm also the guy whose dad left when he was nine without making any arrangements for anything. I, of all people, know what that costs a house.

WES: So you're the last man who should be driving around that light. A nine-year-old who watched a dent go down the street knows what a missing paper costs.

JONATHON: That's the part that sat with me in the truck. I talk like a protector, and I've been leaving the one paper that would actually protect them in a drawer we don't have.

## SEGMENT 2 · Wes and the dent

WES: Can I tell you something embarrassing about my will, because I don't get to sit over here looking tidy.

JONATHON: You have a will. Of course you do. I need the embarrassing part, because if you just have one I'm going to feel like the only idiot at this table.

WES: I've had a will since 2002. My daughter was a baby. My wife did all of it. She found the lawyer, she made the appointment, she filled out the forms at the kitchen table, and I signed where she put the little yellow stickers. I remember the stickers. I didn't know where the will was. I could not have told you who was on it. I found out the guardian was her sister when I was fifty.

JONATHON: So your will was her duffel, and you called it "we have a will" for twenty years. That's me. That's exactly what I would do, and then I'd tell a room I was the one who took care of it.

WES: Most married men I know have a will their wife did, or no will at all. Either way, the question that matters, who raises my kids, a man let somebody else answer. Somebody has to picture it so the kids don't have to live it without a plan. I pictured it late. Fifty years late on the guardian. That's not tidy. That's me.

JONATHON: Okay. That one is going to stick. Now the dent, because you promised a dent you paid for and called grace.

WES: December, at the feed store, my sixteen-year-old backed my truck into a post. The post was built in 1971. The post was fine. My tailgate wasn't. He came in the house white as a sheet. I said, "Don't worry about it." I took it to the body shop. I paid it. I didn't even tell my wife what it cost.

JONATHON: That sounds like grace if I say it fast. What's wrong with it, because I already know I would have done the same thing and told myself I was being a good dad.

WES: Three weeks later he did a rolling stop through a stop sign with me in the truck. When I said something, he said, "It's fine, it's just a truck." Because to him it was. The dent cost him nothing. He never saw the bill. I carried it, so he learned the truck does not weigh anything.

JONATHON: You took his weight and called it love. Some of it was love. A lot of it was you not wanting a hard conversation with a sixteen-year-old, so you paid your way out of it.

WES: Same problem as you. Different pile. You set down one that's yours. I picked up one that's his. Either way the weight ends up in the wrong place.

[BOARD: Same mix-up. Two different piles.]

## SEGMENT 3 · The Word

WES: We've got a little under fifteen minutes, and I want the Bible open, because Paul already sorted the piles. Galatians six, verse five. I brought the glasses. Don't make it a thing.

JONATHON: It's a thing. You only put those on when the verse is about to get us, and I can feel that coming from here.

WES: "for each one should carry their own load."

[BOARD: Proverbs 27:17 (NIV) · "As iron sharpens iron, so one person sharpens another."]

WES: That's the whole verse. It starts with "for" because it's the end of a sentence. Paul is writing to churches that were comparing themselves, a lot of "at least I'm not him." He says quit looking at the next man's life. Look at your own work. Each one carries his own load.

JONATHON: Okay, hold on. That's the same chapter that tells you to pick up what the other man can't carry. Three verses earlier. So which is it, because if I'm a guy on my commute I just heard two opposite orders.

WES: Both. I've been helping people move for thirty years, so this is my expertise. Every man carries his own duffel. Nobody carries a couch alone. Your duffel is your work, your words, your marriage, your kids, your health, your walk with God. Nobody can carry that for you, and you don't hand it to your wife and call it teamwork. A couch is the thing that's too heavy for one man. A death. A diagnosis. A son in trouble. A job gone. When a man's got a couch, his brothers show up and grab a corner. If you have been doing both jobs, yours and his, you will feel holy about it until your back goes out.

JONATHON: And the problem is, men get those backwards. We try to carry the couch alone, because asking feels like failing. Then we hand our duffel to somebody else, because it's boring and it's been sitting by the door for three years. The will is a duffel. The dent was your son's duffel, and you carried it like a couch.

WES: That's the question I want you sitting with. What duffel have you been leaving by the door? And whose couch are you trying to carry by yourself? Look around your house this week and name both before you get cute about either one.

## SEGMENT 4 · Pushback

WES: Jonathon, you've been sitting on something. Push.

JONATHON: Here's my problem with that. This can sound like bootstraps. Every man for himself. Carry your own load, don't complain, don't need anybody. I grew up with a mom who did everything by herself, and I watched what that did to her. I'm not sure "carry your own load" is good news. It can sound like pride with a verse taped on it.

WES: That's fair. Paul isn't handing you a slogan about going it alone. He is saying nobody else is responsible for your part. Those are different. Your mom carried her duffel and your dad's duffel and a couch or two, for years, alone. That's not what Paul means. That's what happens when a man doesn't pick his up.

JONATHON: That's my dad. He set his down, and my mom picked it up, and she's still carrying some of it. I never heard the verse on her side. Most people hear "don't ask for help." It's really "don't make somebody else carry what's yours."

WES: Second one. What are you carrying that isn't yours?

JONATHON: My mom's feelings. Every holiday. That's the fight Dani and I had in October that we finish on Sunday. Every Thanksgiving, every Christmas, every Easter, the whole schedule gets built around whether my mom is going to be okay. If she's alone, if she's sad, if she feels left out. I've been carrying that since I was nine. She told me I was the man of the house the week he left. She was thirty-one with two kids. She was overwhelmed. I heard it, and I took it, and I've been carrying her happiness ever since.

WES: Honoring your mother is yours. That's in the Ten Commandments. Calling her, seeing her, making sure she's got what she needs, that's your duffel. Whether she's happy on Christmas afternoon isn't yours. That one belongs to her and God. Can you actually carry it? Have you ever once made her happy on a holiday by moving your schedule around?

JONATHON: No. She's still sad. We're just tired, and Dani's mad. That's how you know it isn't mine. When I carry something that's mine, somebody gets lighter. When I carry something that isn't, nobody gets lighter. I just get heavier.

[BOARD: If nobody gets lighter, it is not yours.]

WES: Last one, and this is the man who can't. He lost the job, or he's low, or his body does not work right, and "pick up your load" sounds like one more failure.

JONATHON: Yeah, because I can hear that guy from here, and I don't want us to crush him with a moving metaphor.

WES: His load today might be getting out of bed and calling one person. That counts. Picking up your weight is never "be the guy you were before." It's "carry what's yours today." Sometimes what's yours today is the phone call where you say I can't carry this alone. That's not dropping the load. That's picking it up.

JONATHON: So for a man who's barely standing, asking for help is the heaviest thing he'll lift all week. I'll take that. Now you've got one for me. I can feel it.

WES: Who pays the bills at your house?

JONATHON: Dani. Dani does all the money. She's better at it. She's got a spreadsheet with colors. I'm not allowed to touch the spreadsheet. If she couldn't do it for three months, could I pay the mortgage next month? I don't know the number. I know the neighborhood. I don't know the day. I don't know the password. That's a duffel. I came in with one and I'm leaving with two.

WES: Dividing the work is fine. You don't have to do the spreadsheet. A man ought to know where the weight is in his own house, so if the person carrying it goes down, he can pick it up the same day.

## SEGMENT 5 · What we actually did

WES: We're about halfway through the last stretch. What did you actually do?

JONATHON: Sunday night I found a lawyer. There's a woman at our church who does wills. I knew, because she announced it at a men's breakfast once and I avoided eye contact. Monday morning I called her office. Thursday, four-thirty. I put it on the calendar as "Will. 4:30." Short, so I could not argue with it.

WES: And Dani?

JONATHON: I told her at supper. She laughed. Not mean. Surprised. Then, "Who's watching the kids Thursday?" Fair. I hadn't thought about that. Then, "We have to decide the kids before Thursday." I said, "I know." That's the part I haven't done yet. Wednesday, after bath. Sunday is the other talk. It's a big week in the kitchen.

WES: And one small one, because I said look for the dent.

JONATHON: Ruby's supposed to make her bed. That's her one job. Every morning, while she's eating toast, I go in and make it for her, because it takes me forty seconds and it takes her eleven minutes and a negotiation. She's seven, and she has never once made her bed on a school day. Today I didn't. We were four minutes late. She was furious. The bed looked like a raccoon slept in it. I've never been prouder of a terrible bed.

WES: That's a good bed. I sat him down Saturday with the actual bill. I said, "I was wrong to just pay this. Not because you're in trouble. Because it's yours, and I took it from you." He looked at the number a long time. Then he said, "That's a lot of money." I said, "Yes, it is."

JONATHON: So he's paying you back, and I want the part about your brother, because you don't get to skip the "Well" man after you finally called him.

WES: I called my older brother. I said, "Could you use a sixteen-year-old on Saturdays?" Long quiet. Then, "Send him." So my son spends Saturdays at my brother's place, feeding cattle and fixing fence. Twelve Saturdays. I drive him out and I pick him up. Forty minutes of truck each way, and two cups of coffee in my brother's kitchen. I didn't plan that part.

JONATHON: That's God being funny with trucks. You also took the Tuesday drives. Your wife had been carrying those since August.

WES: I took them in January. That was my duffel, and my wife had been carrying it since August while I told myself I was busy. Mom counts my reps. I'm the driver. I'm not doing the exercises. She counts them anyway. Last week she said, "You're limping worse than me." Appointment is tomorrow. Ten in the morning. I put it on the calendar the way you put the will on yours, short, so I can't talk myself out of the parking lot.

JONATHON: Are you nervous, because you put this off since October, and I don't think it was laziness.

WES: Yeah. My dad had both knees done in his sixties, and he was never the same. He went from the man who fixed everything to the man in the chair by the window. Somewhere in my head I decided if nobody says the word "knee" out loud, I don't have to become him. The limp was safer than the answer. That's backwards. He waited too. On that, too. I want you to hear that, because a lot of us are waiting on a doctor and calling it being fine. Fine is just a quieter way to leave the duffel by the door.

JONATHON: So you're picking up a weight your dad set down. I'll text you at four. I'm not texting you during it. I'm not a monster.

[BOARD: The limp was never cheaper than the answer.]

## SEGMENT 6 · This week's challenge

WES: Last few minutes on the challenge. Two parts, both small. I want them small on purpose, because a man will use a big assignment as another reason to wait. One step is enough to put your hand on the weight. You can finish the rest after Friday.

JONATHON: Part one. Find one weight with your name on it that you've been letting somebody else carry. Put your hand on it before Friday. One real step. A phone call, an appointment, a hard sentence.

WES: Part two. Find one thing you're carrying that isn't yours. You don't have to set it down yet. Name it, and write down whose it actually is. Friday we've got a tool for the setting-down part.

JONATHON: Married men, ask your wife this on a calm night, not while she's in the middle of carrying it. "What do you carry around here that's really mine?" Then don't argue with the answer. Write it down. Did you ask yours yet, or are you waiting for a calmer night than this one?

WES: I asked. She said birthdays. Twenty-seven years. If you live alone, nobody's carrying it for you. It's just sitting there. Start with the budget, the doctor, and your emergency contact. If you had to think about the contact, that's your duffel.

JONATHON: And if you're a parent, look for the dent. What have you been paying for that your kid should be carrying? Age-appropriate. Don't hand a six-year-old the mortgage. Theo would lose the mortgage in the backyard and then ask for a snack.

WES: Divorced dads, the weight with your name on it's your time with your kids and what you bring to it. You can't carry what happens at her house. Your weekends are completely yours, and they count. Don't spend them trying to win a fight you already left. Spend them being the man who showed up.
