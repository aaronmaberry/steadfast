**Arc.** *Hook:* Theo asks what a man is, and Jonathon says "a big boy." *Tension:* most men never got a sentence, so they live a bad one they never read. Wes's diner receipt. Jonathon writes "Win the room" on Wes's feed-store receipt. Coach, church, phone. The world is loud everywhere except your table. Genesis 1:27. *Turn:* your kids are already writing theirs by watching you. *Resolution:* write the one you've been living, tell one man, Friday they check.

## SEGMENT 2 · The sentence you've been living

WES: Here's where I have to tell on myself, because I didn't have a sentence for a long time either. Or I did, and it was a bad one.

JONATHON: What was yours, because I need to know I'm not the only man at this table who would fail a four-year-old.

WES: About a year into meeting with Mr. Abernathy, he slid a diner receipt across the table, flipped it over, and handed me a pen. And he said, "Write down what you think a man is. Not what you'd say at church. What you've actually been doing."

JONATHON: That's a lineman move. They don't do soft, and they don't let you hide behind a good Sunday sentence.

WES: I sat there a while. And I wrote, "Pay the bills and don't make trouble." That was it. Fifteen years. I paid every bill. I never made any trouble. I also never made anything else.

JONATHON: Where did you get it? Because that doesn't sound like something a man invents on a Tuesday. That sounds like something he inherits.

WES: From my dad, mostly. Not from anything he said. From what he didn't say. He was a good man. He paid the bills. He didn't make trouble. And he never once sat me down and said, "Son, here's what a man is." I watched what he did, and I wrote my own sentence from the outside of his life.

JONATHON: Paying the bills matters. The second half is where a man disappears.

WES: "Don't make trouble" was really "don't step into anything." Don't have the hard conversation. Don't lead at home. Stay in the recliner and keep the lights on. Nothing in that sentence tells you to get up.

JONATHON: So that's where the recliner came from. A thin sentence lets a man sit down and call it being responsible.

WES: Mr. Abernathy read it, folded it, put it in his shirt pocket, and said, "Well, that's honest. Next time you can write a better one." I didn't, for a while. When he died, his daughter found it in his Bible, with a bunch of other ones. Thirty-some receipts. Thirty-some men. All with bad sentences on them. He'd kept every one.

## SEGMENT 3 · Your turn

WES: Which brings me to you. You asked me where I got mine. Write yours. The one you've actually been living. Not the bathtub one.

JONATHON: Right here? You're going to make me do this with a pen, in front of people, on a piece of paper you pulled out of your pocket.

WES: Right here. Whose receipt is this? Feed store. Saturday. Two bags of deer corn and a salt block.

JONATHON: Of course it is. That's the most Wes thing you could slide across a table. Give me a minute. I'm not joking while I do this.

WES: Take your time. If you're in the truck, he's writing. It's taking him a minute. That's a good sign.

JONATHON: [sets the pen down] "Win the room." That's it. That's what I've been doing since I was about twelve. Every room I walk into. Customers, church, the coach-pitch parents. I'm reading everybody, figuring out what they want, and I'm making sure they like me before I leave.

WES: Where'd you get it?

JONATHON: I've never said this out loud. I think I got it the year my dad left. Because if he could leave, anybody could leave. And the way you make sure people don't leave is you make them like you. You win the room. Every room. Every time.

WES: [quietly] Yeah.

JONATHON: And it works. That's the problem. It made me a great salesman. It did not make me a good husband for a while, because Dani doesn't need me to win her. She's already in the room. She needed me to stay in it when I wasn't winning.

WES: That's the long way home on 1604.

JONATHON: That's 1604. Bath time is a room I can't win. A four-year-old flooding the bathroom doesn't care if he likes me. So I skipped it. I'm keeping this receipt. If the lineman kept yours, I'm keeping mine the same way.

## SEGMENT 4 · Where they come from

WES: We're about halfway, and I want a few minutes on where men pick these up, because I don't think it's always a dad. There's also a coach, a boss, a church, and now the phone.

JONATHON: The coach one is real. My pitching coach in college had one sentence he said about a hundred times a season. "Never let them see you hurt." My shoulder hurt for two months before I told anybody. By the time I told the trainer, it was done. That was my last season. I was twenty.

WES: So the sentence cost you your arm.

JONATHON: The sentence cost me my arm. He was a good coach. He got that sentence from somebody too. But I carried it another ten years. Into my marriage. Into my drinking. You don't tell anybody you're hurting. You have two more beers on a Tuesday and call it unwinding.

WES: The church one is subtler. A lot of men pick up "a good man is a nice man." Be nice, be quiet, don't make anybody uncomfortable. That overlaps a lot with "don't make trouble." That's where I got the second half of mine.

JONATHON: So people in a church handed you a thin one, and you wore it like it came from the Bible.

WES: People in a church handed me a thin one. Jesus was kind. He wasn't nice the way we mean nice. He flipped tables. He told the truth to people who didn't want it. The boss gives you "produce." Your value is your output.

JONATHON: That's mine, partly. Win the room, hit the number. And the phone gives you whatever sells. A twenty-two-year-old is guessing from a feed, and the feed will sell him a new sentence every week.

[BOARD: Proverbs 20:6 (NIV) · "Many claim to have unfailing love, but a faithful person who can find?"]

WES: So nobody's walking around with no sentence. Every man is walking around with one he stitched together from a coach, a boss, a dad, and a phone, and he's never once read it out loud.

## SEGMENT 5 · Loud and quiet

WES: If you don't have a sentence, you're not broken. You're not crazy. The confusion isn't only in you. It's in the air.

JONATHON: Okay, so here's where I want to push back a little. The way you talk about it, the world went quiet on manhood. And I don't think it's quiet. I think it's screaming.

WES: Say more.

JONATHON: My feed has a hundred answers. A guy telling me a man makes six figures, lifts, and doesn't apologize. Another guy telling me masculinity is the problem with everything. A guy in a cold plunge. A guy selling me a course. It's not quiet. It's the loudest topic on my phone.

WES: [beat] That's fair. I'll take the correction. It's loud. Here's what I'd say, though. It's loud everywhere you don't live. It's quiet at your table.

[BOARD: A feed talks at a crowd. A table talks to a name.]

JONATHON: So it's not that nobody's talking. It's that nobody's talking to me. Not one of those guys knows my name. Not one of them has watched me parent. Not one of them is going to sit across from me on a Thursday.

WES: A sentence you get from a feed is a costume. A sentence you get at a table is a fit. The thing that's missing is a man who knows you saying it to your face.

JONATHON: I'll buy the costume. I still think you should say loud, because "quiet" lets a guy think nobody cares, when too many people care and none of them know him.

WES: Duly noted.

JONATHON: He says "duly noted" when he's not going to change it, and I need that on the record.

WES: That's what duly noted means.

## SEGMENT 6 · The Word

WES: So if the noise doesn't give a man his sentence, where does he start? He starts before the noise. Genesis one. Before anybody had an opinion. God makes people, and the very first thing the Bible says about a human being is this.

JONATHON: "So God created mankind in his own image, in the image of God he created them; male and female he created them."

WES: Two sentences of context. This is the top of the whole story. Before sin, God says what we are. We're made in his image, male and female, on purpose.

JONATHON: So what does that do for the guy in the bathtub? Because "image of God" sounds big, and I've got a four-year-old with a shark.

WES: It tells him he's not making it up. Being a man isn't a costume you buy off the internet. It's a design. Somebody made you on purpose. So when you feel that hunger for a sentence, that's not you being insecure. That's a design looking for its instructions.

[BOARD: The hunger is not a defect.]

JONATHON: That would have helped me at twenty-two. I thought I was faking it. I thought every other guy had a sentence in his pocket and I was the only one guessing.

WES: People hear "image" and think two arms, two legs. In the world Genesis was written in, a king put up a statue of himself in a city he ruled. That was his image. It meant, this place belongs to him, and this is what he's like. When God says he made people in his image, he's saying, you're the statue. You're the thing in the world that shows what I'm like.

JONATHON: So a man isn't just made. He's made to show something. And a man living "pay the bills and don't make trouble" isn't showing much. He's a statue facing the wall.

WES: That's me in the recliner. I'm allowed to be mean to that guy. That's why you're not crazy. You're supposed to want it. If you were made by somebody, it makes sense you'd want to know what for.

JONATHON: Okay, but I have to ask this one, because somebody's thinking it. Does this turn into "men are better"? Because the second you start talking about what a man is, somebody hears "and women are less."

WES: Look at the verse. Same breath. "Male and female he created them." Both in the image. There's no ranking in that sentence. A man wanting a sentence doesn't take anything from a woman. My wife doesn't need me to be less of a man so she can be more of a woman. She needs me to show up.

JONATHON: Dani would say the same thing. Louder. And better. She would not let me turn this into a ranking.

## SEGMENT 7 · Pushback

WES: Jonathon, you've been sitting on a real objection. Go ahead and push.

JONATHON: Why do I need a sentence at all? Can't I just be a good person? Be kind, work hard, go to church, love my kids? Why does it have to be "a man"? I've sat through enough lists.

WES: Being a good person matters. I'm not going to argue with kind. But if you don't decide what a man is, the loudest voice in your life decides for you.

JONATHON: Like the feed. Or like your dad's silence. You don't get to opt out. You just get to choose whether you looked at it.

WES: Everybody's got one. Most men have just never read theirs. And Theo's getting one right now. From you. Your son isn't waiting for you to have a good answer. He's writing his sentence by watching you.

JONATHON: [sits back] Well, that's terrifying. He watched me say "be a man about it" to a crying four-year-old. That's in his sentence now, and I can't pull it back.

WES: Then you put a better line next to it. You don't erase the old ones. You write over them.

JONATHON: Let me do the other seasons. The twenty-two-year-old is guessing from podcasts. The sixty-two-year-old thinks it's too late. The guy like me thinks a title is a definition. What do you say to them?

WES: The twenty-two-year-old is early, not behind. Find a man twenty years older and ask him what his sentence was. The sixty-two-year-old still can change it, and his grown sons are taking notes. And your title isn't a definition. Regional sales manager is a business card. When it goes away, a man who only had a title doesn't have anything left to say about himself.

JONATHON: You keep doing that to me, and I keep walking into it on purpose.

WES: I keep doing it to myself first. Steadfast Men has a chapter on this. Chapter two. The book's at walksteadfast.com if you want the longer version of the same table.

## SEGMENT 8 · What we actually did

WES: Last few minutes on what we actually did, because I don't want to hand you a challenge I haven't touched. This week I told my youngest my old sentence.

JONATHON: The sixteen-year-old. The one who just got his license. You were holding the handle above the door again, weren't you.

WES: I was holding the handle. I said, "Can I tell you something embarrassing?" He said, "Is it about your knees?" I said no. I told him about the receipt. Fifteen years I thought a man paid the bills and didn't make trouble.

JONATHON: What did he say, because a sixteen-year-old will either give you nothing or give you the whole report card.

WES: He was quiet for about a mile. Then he said, "That's kind of what I thought you thought." He'd read it off of me. I said, "I'm working on a better one." He said, "Okay." And then he ran a yellow light.

JONATHON: How deep? Because "yellow" in his mouth and "yellow" in your mouth are not the same color.

WES: It was a dark yellow. What about you?

JONATHON: Next bath night I said, "Hey buddy, remember when you asked me what a man is?" He didn't remember. He'd been carrying a shark. I'd been carrying it six days. I said it anyway. "A man is somebody who takes care of people, even when he's tired." He said, "Are you tired?" I said, "Always, buddy." And he patted my head with a wet hand, like I was the kid.

[BOARD: Put the real one on paper.]

WES: You said it out loud to your son. First time. A sentence about what a man is.

JONATHON: First time. Thirty-six years old. A wet hand on my head, and a sentence that isn't a fortune cookie.

## SEGMENT 9 · This week's challenge

WES: So here's your challenge this week. Write down the sentence you've actually been living. Not the church one. The real one. Especially if it's a bad one. Mine was "pay the bills and don't make trouble." Yours might be "keep everybody happy." Or "don't need anybody." Or "win." Write it on something. A receipt works.

JONATHON: And then tell one man. Same guy you texted if that went well. Read it to him. Don't clean it up first.

WES: If you're married, ask your wife what she thinks your sentence is. Pick a calm night. Say yours first. If her answer stings, don't swing back. Write it down next to yours and sit with it for a day.

JONATHON: If you're single, find the friend who's seen you at your worst. Ask him, "When things go sideways, what do I do?" Whatever he says, that's your sentence talking. Then offer him his.

WES: If there's nobody to ask, write it anyway and read it out loud to God. He's known you the longest. It takes five minutes. Do it this week.
