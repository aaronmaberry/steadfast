## SEGMENT 1 · The driveway and the tie

WES: Let's take the next few minutes on two handoffs, one that happened and one that never did. August. I was eighteen. Everything I owned in a Buick. Three hundred miles. My dad shook my hand at breakfast, said drive careful, and went to the barn.
JONATHON: He said drive careful and went to the barn. Yeah, and—your mom brought the last load, warm and folded. Then she went inside.
WES: My mom brought the last load, warm and folded, put it in the back seat, put her hand on the roof, and said the line I opened with. Then she went inside. No wave.

JONATHON: She was handing you to your own life. I keep waiting for a sentence like that and I get a FaceTime from a man on her porch instead.

WES: I came home every weekend the first semester with laundry. She did it without a word. She had told me not to, and then she loved me anyway. After the pot roast she asked me to bring a tape measure in May, to see if her china cabinet fits the apartment. That's a woman who is trying to leave a house on purpose.

JONATHON: I made a list this week of the men who almost counted me in. My mom called me man of the house. That was a job, not a handoff. The pitching coach said, "Never let them see you hurt." The Beaumont manager, same tie every Monday, his best line was "Not bad, kid." The thing I worked for called me kid.

WES: And last month you said it to your own rep. Did you hear the old manager in your mouth before he floated out, or after?

JONATHON: After. Thirty-one. Good at his job. I looked over his screen and said, "Not bad." He floated out of the office. I had become the tie. I sat there and thought, nobody ever told me I was a man. They told me I was useful.
WES: [laughs] They told you you were useful. All right. Those aren't the same sentence as a blessing. Keep going.
JONATHON: Those aren't the same sentence. I can still hear the Beaumont office, the same Monday tie, the way I waited on those two words like they were a blessing. They were a grade. A grade keeps you coming back. A blessing lets you leave.

[BOARD: A job is not a blessing.]

WES: See whether home has handed you off yet. If you're still useful to your mother and you have never been released, you will pass that down. You will hire men and call them kid. You will love your people by staying in charge of them.
JONATHON: Yeah. You keep your people by staying in charge of them. What'd you do with your own boys, because I heard chores in that.
WES: I did a version of that with my own boys. I handed them chores and called it forming them. Sometimes it was. Sometimes it was me needing a guy in the room so I wouldn't feel the quiet.

## SEGMENT 2 · The capacitor

WES: Tell them about Saturday, because that's the picture. Where were you when she called?

JONATHON: Dugout. My mom called. "The man's coming at ten. Can you be on the phone?" I took the FaceTime in the Bluebonnets dugout, three and a half hours away. The AC man took the phone and talked to me, not to her, in the house she has owned twenty-two years.
WES: In the house she has owned twenty-two years. That's the bump. The AC man talked to you, not to her. What did you do with your hands while he talked past her?
JONATHON: I held the phone like a desk. She looked relieved. I've sat at the dealership desk in Pasadena for every car she has bought since I was sixteen. I don't know how to be her son. I only know how to be her guy.

WES: That's a kind sentence and a hard one. She's not the villain. She asked the person who has always answered. You answered because that's how you love her. The string isn't her fault alone.

JONATHON: Sunday I called her back. I said, "Next time the AC man comes, I want you to talk to him. Then call me after." She said, "You don't want to come?" I said I want to come Sunday and eat with her. I don't want to come for the capacitor. She said she does not know what a capacitor is.
WES: She doesn't know what a capacitor is. [beat] Yeah. And you said, Mom, I don't either. She laughed.
JONATHON: I said, "Mom, I don't either." She laughed. Then she said okay. In my family, okay means hurt a little and going to try. I hung up and sat in the driveway a minute, because I had just taken a job away from myself and I didn't know where to put my hands.

WES: That's a handoff. Small. Honest. You didn't slam a door. You moved one job off your chest and left the relationship on the table.

JONATHON: Ruby, Sunday night, bedtime. I said my grandfather's name out loud for the first time. She said it back twice. "Is he my great-grandpa?" "Is he alive?" No. "What did he do?" The rigs, and fishing. Then she said, "Can WE go fishing?" I said I don't know how to fish. She said, "You could learn."

WES: My older brother's stock tank is full of bass. There is a dock. Ruby and Theo can come. Dani can judge from the shade. I'm offering. You can say maybe. I'm still offering.

JONATHON: Maybe. I heard myself say I don't know how, and it sounded like my dad's rods all over again. I don't want to be the man who never takes them because nobody took him.

[BOARD: The dock is still an offer.]

WES: Saturday the Bluebonnets lost twelve to three. Their best hitter throws like she's mad at the ball. Ruby, in the truck: "Daddy, you can be sad. We lost a lot." She said my coach voice was sad. Truck Saturday eleven is done. One left. I like the drive and I won't say that where my son can hear it. I've got one more Saturday at seven in the morning, and I bought the habit of looking forward to a boy who used to think I only showed up to inspect work. I don't want that Saturday to end, and I also don't want to keep him as my guy.

## SEGMENT 3 · The Word

WES: Luke stays open for about fifteen minutes. Chapter two. Jesus is twelve. His parents have lost him in Jerusalem. They find him in the temple. Verse forty-nine.

JONATHON: "Why were you searching for me?" he asked. "Didn't you know I had to be in my Father's house?"

[BOARD: Luke 2:51 (NIV) · "Then he went down to Nazareth with them and was obedient to them."]

WES: Quick context. He's not slamming a door on Mary and Joseph. The next line has him going home under their roof again. He names a Father and a house that aren't Joseph's shop, and then he honors the people who raised him. Leaving and honoring in the same breath. That's the tension.

JONATHON: So he's not cruel to his mother. He's clear. I've been useful and unclear. I've come home for every capacitor and called that honor. It was fear. If I'm not her guy, I don't know what I'm for. So which is it? Stay useful at twelve, or name your own house and still drive to Pasadena?

WES: Name your own house. You're her son. That's a different job. A son calls. A son eats. A son does not have to run the service call from a dugout three hours away. Joseph still got a son who came home. He didn't get a son who stayed twelve and useful forever. I think a lot of us stay twelve on purpose. Twelve is needed. Grown is chosen. Needed feels safer, because if they still need you they can't leave, and you can't be left.

[BOARD: 1 Corinthians 13:11 (NIV) · "When I became a man, I put the ways of childhood behind me."]

JONATHON: Handoff language still sounds too clean for a capacitor house. Luke is the Son of God in the temple. I'm a sales guy whose mom needs a capacitor. If I use this verse to stop showing up, I become the man who left with a lamp. I won't do that. Wait, back up. Are you telling me to skip the service call, or to stop running it from a dugout?

WES: Stop running it from the dugout. Nobody is asking you to leave with a lamp. We're asking you to stop confusing showing up with staying in charge. Jesus named the Father's house and then went to Nazareth. You can name your own house, Dani and the kids, and still drive to Pasadena for pot pie.

## SEGMENT 4 · Pushback

WES: Jonathon, push. I can hear the men who think we're telling them to drop their mothers. Go ahead.

JONATHON: Yeah, here's my problem. The guy whose mom is a widow and actually needs him. "You want me to tell her to talk to the AC man? She will get taken." That's a real fear. I've sat at those desks because I've watched men talk past her.

WES: Then you teach her the questions, and you stay on the porch, not on the phone. Honor isn't doing the conversation for her every time. Honor is making sure she's not alone in a room with a stranger if she does not want to be, and then letting her speak. If she can't, you're there. If she can, you stop stealing the sentence.

JONATHON: That's fair, but— next is the guy who lives ten minutes away. He thinks this does not apply. He sees her every Sunday. I lived that. I bought a house with a better yard that happened to be ten minutes from her. Seeing her isn't the same as being handed off. You can eat roast every week and still be the man of a house that's not yours. Distance isn't the test. Wait, back up. Are we saying Sunday roast is a hide if you still run the table?

WES: If you still run the table, yes. The single guy whose mom still does his laundry. He thinks he's being a good son by letting her. He's being a boy who is convenient. I did my own wash after that first semester because I got tired of the drive, not because I got holy. Sometimes the handoff starts as laziness and then becomes a life. Take the life. Leave the excuse.

JONATHON: Put your own laundry in. Call her about her week, not about your socks. If the only reason you go home is a basket, you have not been handed off. You have been folded.

WES: And the man whose dad never left, but still never said he was a man. My dad shook my hand and went to the barn. My mom had to say it. Some of you're waiting for a barn door to open. It may not. You may have to receive the sentence from the person who will actually say it, and then live like it's true.

JONATHON: I'll be real. Part of me wants a ceremony. A driveway. A blessing. I didn't get one. I got a job at eleven and a tie that said not bad. I don't fully buy that I can hand myself off. I think I still want someone else to do it.

WES: You can want that and still make the Sunday call. Wanting a ceremony isn't a reason to stay on the FaceTime. I wanted my dad to come out of the barn that morning. He didn't. My mom said the sentence. I've spent years being a little mad at the barn and a little grateful for the roof of that Buick. Both of those can stay. The call still has to happen.

## SEGMENT 5 · The bravest thing

WES: We've got about seven minutes, and I want them on you. The truck. The nine minutes. "Not yet." I said it to you on the way in, and I'm saying it here. That wasn't a kid. That's the bravest thing I've watched a man do this year.

JONATHON: [beat] I don't know what to do with that. I keep wanting to joke it away. I sat in a driveway and asked a man about his dad. It does not feel like courage. It feels like I was late. I would rather you call it late than put a medal on it. I can live with late. I can't live with being called brave for doing the first ordinary thing a son should have done at sixteen.

WES: Late and brave can sit in the same truck. You were late. You still dialed. I've watched men stay useful and never become sons. You're trying to become a son at thirty-seven, and you're trying to stop being a kid at work. Both at once. That's weight, and it's the kind that actually belongs on a grown man.

JONATHON: Theo got lost at the Rodeo for four minutes when he was three, by the funnel cakes. We found him explaining something to a goat. I think about that when I talk about being handed off. He wasn't trying to run the Rodeo. He was just a boy talking to a goat. I never got to be that unuseful. I want my kids to get a few years of being a kid before somebody makes them the guy.

WES: Then give them that. And give your mom a Sunday that's not a work order. And take the fishing offer or don't, but don't say you can't learn. Ruby already told you that you could.

JONATHON: I heard her. I'm still saying maybe, because I don't want to turn your brother's dock into a project I've got to win. I want it to be a Saturday. That's new for me.

WES: A Saturday is enough. The dock man has no idea he's teaching you yet. He will stand there with a coffee can of worms and say evening. That's a handoff too, from a man who isn't your dad. You don't have to make it a project with a score. You have to show up and let a seven-year-old bait a hook badly. That's how a house learns a skill the last man never passed down.

## SEGMENT 6 · This week's challenge

WES: So here is your challenge. If your mom still calls you for the capacitor, pick one job this week and hand it back. Not all of them. One. Tell her you want to come eat, and you want her to talk to the man, and then you want her to call you after. Write the sentence before you dial so you don't slide back into being the guy the minute she sounds tired. Has home handed you off, or are you still waiting? Will you hand it back this Sunday, or slide the minute she sounds tired?

JONATHON: I'll hand it back. That's fair, but— I've got a confession for Friday, and it's not pretty. For about ten years Dani has returned Sunday calls I didn't want to sit through. I let her be the voice so I could stay the guy. Married men, tell your wife which job you're handing back. Single men, put your own laundry in and ask your mom one question about her week that's not about a bill.

WES: If your mom has died, ask somebody who knew her what she wanted for you when you left. Write it down. If she never said it, write the sentence you needed and stop waiting for a ghost to say it. Then live the sentence. Don't use the missing ceremony as a reason to stay twelve.

JONATHON: And if you're the dad of a son who is about to leave, say the sentence out loud. You're a man now. Act like one. Come home for dinner, not for a work order. Don't make your wife say it from the roof of a Buick because you went to the barn. I want that on a card for me, for Theo, eight years out. I don't want him to learn how to be my guy. I want him to learn how to be a son.
