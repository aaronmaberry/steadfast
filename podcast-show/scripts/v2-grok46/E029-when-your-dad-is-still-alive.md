## SEGMENT 1 · The wave and the boots

WES: Let's take the next few minutes on this weekend, because the topic is already sitting on two tables. You first. Saturday. The Bluebonnets.

JONATHON: We lost six to five. Three games by a combined three runs, and I keep saying that to the parents like it means we're close. We're a team that loses by one and then talks about math.

WES: [laughs] It's a stat. It's just a stat for a team that keeps losing.

JONATHON: Last inning Ruby stands on second and waves at Dani like a parade float. Then she's out, the game is over, and she asks if I saw the wave. I said, "Great wave." Then I sat in the truck and thought about a man I've never called.

WES: Saturday night my oldest came at seven with steel-toe work boots, drywall mud and something orange. Not church shoes. I almost said those aren't the kind you polish, and I caught it, because that's exactly my dad.

JONATHON: So you bought a newspaper on purpose, farm section, because nobody gets one anymore, and you sat down anyway.

WES: We sat an hour. About forty words. The Astros. A noise in his truck. I kept thinking, say something. About forty-five minutes in he says, without looking up, "Did Grandpa ever tell you he was proud of you?" I said, "Once. In a hospital bed." He said, "Oh." Then he went back to the boot.

JONATHON: He asked you, and you answered about your dad, and you left the real sentence sitting there with the polish.

WES: He washed the truck before his uncle's, nobody asked him to. Two Saturdays left. That visor paper is still stuck to the fridge. I walk past it like a deer in the yard.

JONATHON: Two weeks ago my VP sent five words. "Can we talk about Q2." I lost a Wednesday morning. Thursday it was a territory change. Two counties out, two counties back. A scheduling email. I had written him four careful emails, no exclamation points, auditioning. Thursday night I'm brushing my teeth and it lands. Four emails for a man who wanted counties. Zero calls to my father in twenty-eight years. Every call we've ever had, he dialed.

[BOARD: A weather report is not a father.]

WES: Well. That's today. When your dad is still alive. The mark is go first, and you just set yourself up, and I want credit for not saying it first. A man will polish four emails for a boss and leave his father's name sitting on a phone. I've done my version of that for years. I just called it being busy.

## SEGMENT 2 · Twice a year

WES: So tell me about the calls. Not the summary. What happens on one.

JONATHON: Birthday and Christmas. That's the whole schedule. This past Christmas he didn't call. He sent the friend request instead. I accepted it later. He liked a picture. Still no real conversation. The phone still says "Dad." I've never been able to change it to his name.

WES: And when that word lights up, what do you actually do with it?

JONATHON: About half the time I let it go to voicemail. "Hey, it's your dad. Happy birthday. Give me a call sometime." And I don't. The other half I pick up. "Hey, bud." Weather. "How's work?" "Good." "How are the little ones?" "Good." Then a space. Then, "Well. I'll let you go." Five minutes. Six if there was a hurricane.

WES: Tell them the hurricane, because that's the one that still bothers you.

JONATHON: 2020. I watched Lake Charles on the news for two days and didn't call. I texted my sister, "Have you heard from Dad?" Neither of us called. Christmas he told me the roof was off three weeks and he slept in his truck. I said, "Wow." He said, "Yeah, it was something." Six minutes. I used to time them like that was evidence I was right.

WES: Evidence that staying quiet made you the better man.

JONATHON: Then you told the story about the Thursday-table man who texts his son every Sunday. Eight months. No answer. I was lying awake and I thought, I've been the seventeen-year-old. Twenty-eight years. Twice a year he dials, and I answer with the weather.

WES: [beat] Be careful. That man didn't put a lamp in a truck. Twice a year isn't fatherhood.

JONATHON: I know. I won't make him a hero for dialing a phone. But he dialed it fifty-some times. He's the only one of the two of us who ever has. Sitting on my end didn't make me better. It made me the one who never called. When he says he will let me go, I want to say "Don't," or "You already did." Those are the only two answers I've ever had.

## SEGMENT 3 · The Word

WES: Open Exodus with me for about fifteen minutes, because this one is yours. Chapter twenty, verse twelve. The fifth of the Ten Commandments. First one about people, after the ones about God.

JONATHON: "Honor your father and your mother, so that you may live long in the land the Lord your God is giving you."

[BOARD: Luke 15:20 (NIV) · "So he got up and went to his father."]

WES: And your problem is the word honor. You have heard it as pretend. Stand up at the cookout. You can't do that. He took the lamp.

JONATHON: I won't tell Ruby a halo story about a man she has never met. Pretend honor is how a man lies at his own table with his kids listening. So which is it? A halo, or the right weight?

WES: The right weight. Our pastor said the Hebrew word is heavy. Weight. It does not say give him a halo. It says treat him like he weighs what a father weighs. More than nothing. A lot of men make him weigh nothing so he can't hurt them. Other men let him weigh a ton. One comment knocks the week over.

JONATHON: So the right weight. He did real things, some of them terrible, and he's still the only father I get. I can take him seriously and tell the truth in the same breath. I don't have to say the lamp was fine.

WES: The second half is a promise to a people first. Houses where the old folks get weight tend to hold. Houses where the old man is a weather report teach the kids what you do with a father who has gotten inconvenient.

JONATHON: Ruby asked if he knows about her. She's going to learn what I do with him either way. The file isn't the speech. It's the call I make, or the one I keep not making.

[BOARD: The assignment is weight.]

WES: Look at the number you still haven't dialed. If your dad is still alive, your kids already have that file. They don't need the speech you have been writing. They need to watch you give him the right weight while he's still here to receive it.

## SEGMENT 4 · The pot roast

WES: This verse says father and mother. I don't get the father half anymore. Mine has been gone nine years. So I get the mother half. Sunday was the pot roast.

JONATHON: The ask you and your brother finally put on the calendar after that long call. You were both going to shut up after the question.

WES: Quick version if you're new. My mom is seventy-eight. We each had a plan. His was the downstairs room and a recliner. Mine was her house with more help. Neither of us had asked her.

JONATHON: So after the pot roast, your wife took the kids to see the calves, and you asked, and then you had to stay quiet.

WES: "Mom, what do you want?" Eleven seconds. I counted. My brother started to talk at seven, and I kicked him under the table. [laughs] Fifty-eight years old. Kicked him. She wants the senior apartments behind the church. Her friend Ola lives there. Dominoes. A grocery van. Her own door. She can't see to drive at night. I asked why she never said it. She looked at my brother and said, "Because he bought a chair."

JONATHON: Wait, back up. She had known about the recliner since Christmas and said nothing for two years because he bought a chair? That's the whole shape. You protected her right into a ditch.

WES: Yes. Two years. He said, "Well." Then, "I'm keeping the recliner." She laughed. We will visit the apartments in May. There is a list. She's not on it yet.

[BOARD: Ask her, then give the silence room.]

JONATHON: So honor at that table was eleven seconds. You asked her, and then you let what she wanted be heavier than the plan you had been carrying for two years.

WES: You can be a good son right past your own mother. I had a plan for her and I called it love. Love would have asked her two years ago. Honor, on that Sunday, wasn't a speech. It was a question and then eleven seconds of letting her answer be heavier than mine.

## SEGMENT 5 · Pushback

WES: Jonathon, you've been sitting on the men who are already mad at us. Go ahead and push.

JONATHON: Yeah, here's my problem. First, the guy whose dad was dangerous. "You want me to honor a man who hit my mom? Call him because two men at a table said so?"

WES: No phone call. If he's still dangerous, you don't call him because a show said so. Honor does not mean access. You can give a man weight from a long way away. Pray his name. Get a counselor in the room before you get your father in the room.

JONATHON: Some men honor their father by keeping him away from their kids. That's not dishonor. That's taking him as seriously as he deserves. I need that on the record before anybody walks into a bad house.

WES: On the record. Next is the annoying dad. Cowboys. Sunday. When are you getting a real job.

JONATHON: I'm jealous of that man. I would take annoying. You have fifty-two calls a year. I've got two and the weather. Pick up. Let him complain. Ask him something he has never been asked.

WES: I would give a fair amount for one more call about the weather. The single guy still at home thinks honor does not apply because he already eats at the same table.

JONATHON: You know how he chews. You probably don't know his story. Ask one question about his life before you were born. You might be eating dinner with a stranger you like.

WES: The divorced dad. "My kids barely call me. Now I have to call my father?" Your kids will learn how to treat a far-away dad from somebody. And the older guy whose dad is ninety-four and does not know his name.

JONATHON: Then he doesn't know who you are, and you go anyway. A man at the Thursday table drives to Brenham every Saturday. His dad calls him by his brother's name. He says he's practicing. Somebody will have to sit with him one day. He wants to have shown them how.

WES: That's honor with no receipt. No thank-you. No memory on the other side of the table. He still goes. If you have been waiting for your dad to make it easy, you may be waiting for a man who does not know how. Go first anyway, if he's safe.

## SEGMENT 6 · Not yet

WES: Seven minutes left, and I want them on a question I never asked. At my dad's funeral, my uncle, his younger brother, came up at the graveside and said, "You know your daddy had a scholarship to A&M?" Agriculture. Senior year. My granddad said the place needed him. He never went. He never mentioned it. Not to me. Not to anybody I know of.

JONATHON: Forty-three years you had him, and you never once asked what he wanted to be. He was just Dad. Shoes and games. And then you found the other life at a grave.

WES: I stood there and thought, nobody ever asked him anything either. Not his dad. Not his son. That's why I'm on you about one question. Not a speech. A question.

JONATHON: I don't know anything about my grandfather on my dad's side. Not even his name. I know Louisiana. That's it. My mom says they never visited. My dad is the only man alive who knows. I've had fifty-some calls, and I talked about the weather.

WES: So that's what I would do if I were you. I'm not telling you to forgive him. I'm not telling you to fly to Lake Charles. A question is honest. You don't have to pretend anything to ask a man a question.
JONATHON: You don't have to pretend anything to ask a man a question. Yeah, and—what was your dad like. There's no lie in that.
WES: "What was your dad like?" There is no lie in that. There is a lot of weight in it. You're telling him he's a real person, he had a real father, and you want to know.

JONATHON: And if he says he will let me go, I know what I want to say now. "Not yet." I don't know what happens after that. He might hang up. He might talk about redfish. He might cry. I've never heard him cry. I don't know what he sounds like when he stays.

WES: So put a day on it. Out loud. I already promised the listener a day and an hour.

JONATHON: Wednesday night. Tomorrow. Seven-thirty, after the kids are down. I'm going to sit in the truck in the driveway, because I'm not doing the first one in the house where Ruby can hear it. I will ask what his dad was like. I won't bring up the lamp. I won't give the speech I've been writing since I was nine. Then I will shut up the way you did with your mom.

WES: Are you going to call him while he's still here? If he says he'll let you go, will you actually say not yet, or does the speech come out?

JONATHON: Not yet. That's the line. If the speech comes out, I hung up on myself.

WES: Did you tell Dani, or were you going to surprise her with a man sitting in a dark truck?

JONATHON: I told her last night. She said, "Do you want me in the truck?" I said no. She said, "Okay. I'll leave the porch light on." We're in the driveway. The porch light is twenty feet away. I knew what she meant.

WES: Mine is my oldest. Not about my dad. About him. He asked me Saturday if Grandpa ever told me he was proud of me. I answered about my dad and left the real question on the table with the polish.
JONATHON: [beat] You left the real question on the table with the polish. Stay there. You're going to call him this week, on your lunch.
WES: I'm going to call him this week, on my lunch, and tell him I'm proud of him without a reason attached. I don't have the words yet. I keep thinking the right sentence will arrive if I wait. It won't. I've got to say a clumsy one.

## SEGMENT 7 · This week's challenge

WES: So here is your challenge this week. If your dad is alive, and he's safe, go first. Call him. Don't wait for his birthday or yours. When you call, ask him one question about his life before you.
JONATHON: Yeah. Ask him one question about his life before you. Then let him talk. Don't give him the speech.
WES: What was your dad like? What did you want to be? What was the first car you ever had? One question. Then let him talk. Don't give him the speech. Don't settle the lamp. Stay in the quiet long enough for him to be a person.

JONATHON: And when it gets quiet, don't fill it with the weather. Count to eleven if you have to. Wes's mom took eleven seconds and changed the whole plan. Married men, tell your wife before you call, not after. Not for permission. So you're not doing it alone, and so she knows why you're sitting in the truck.

WES: If you're single, especially if you still live at home or you see him all the time, ask him at the table tonight. Divorced dads, call your father, and let your kids know you did. You're teaching them how to treat a far-away dad.

JONATHON: If your dad is gone, ask somebody who knew him. An uncle, an aunt, his old buddy. Wes found out about a scholarship at a graveside. You might not have to wait that long. And if your dad is dangerous, don't call. Write the question down. Pray his name. Find a counselor to say it to. That's honor too.
