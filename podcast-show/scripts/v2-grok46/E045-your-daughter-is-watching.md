## SEGMENT 1 · Go on and a tape

WES: Let's take the next few minutes on Saturday, because the week already handed us a door. The mechanic did the oil changes, ate two donuts, and wrote ONE MONTH on tape on a single mom's dash. Brake pads. Nobody mentioned Fort Sam. The young man with the bus pass brought the donuts, smaller this time, confirmed. I was there because my brother looked at a watch and released me. That is a heading I did not write. It is still a gift.

JONATHON: Sunday night your older brother called, which he does not do, and said you tell your friend Thursday. Evening. Bring the girl. I have poles. Fishing is Thursday after supper at the stock tank. Ruby already asked if the worm can feel it. I said ask the man. I am leaving the office at three, which my VP will notice, and I am going anyway.

WES: Good. Do not make the worm a theology. Let my brother be the man who answers. You get to bait your own hook and catch nothing. That still counts. Austin texted you first the morning after yes, tacos. A question about his daughter. We will let Friday hold the question. I do not want to steal his ask.

JONATHON: I can feel myself wanting to turn Thursday into a content plan for a seven-year-old. I will not. I will drive. I will let a quiet man with poles do the teaching. I am allowed to be the dad who does not know how to fish. She already told me I could learn.

[BOARD: Go on is a blessing.]


## SEGMENT 2 · Fifteen and the shoulder

WES: When my daughter was fifteen we had Saturday runs to buy feed, twenty minutes each way. I asked the same three questions. School. Friends. Practice. One Saturday she said, Daddy, can you ask me something you do not know the answer to. I said, what do you think about when you are by yourself. She talked the rest of the way home and twenty minutes in the driveway. A friend's parents' divorce. A book she thought was wrong about God. I do not remember the book. I remember the sweet feed in the back and my hands on the wheel doing nothing useful.

JONATHON: You had been giving her a quiz. She asked for a person. I give Ruby quizzes too. How was the watercolor. Did you eat your lunch. I already know the shape of the answer. I am grading. I am not meeting her.

WES: The son-in-law eventually asked her real questions. I am grateful. Sunday I called her and asked what she learned about men from watching me. She said she learned to wait. She still does it. She does it with him. Then she asked what I was thinking about in those years. I said money, losing my job, whether I was any good as her daddy. She said she thought I was mad at her. That is not resolved. I am leaving it unresolved on purpose. I will not tidy a wound I made so the show feels complete.

JONATHON: I needed that left open. I have a version coming with a cereal bowl and I do not want a bow on yours before I tell mine. Keep going. The funeral processions. I want that, because it is how a girl learns what a man does when nobody is performing.

WES: My dad pulled onto the shoulder for every funeral procession. Hat on his knee. He would say, somebody's daddy. My sons still pull over. I still pull over. It is a small liturgy. A daughter watches that. She learns that death is not an inconvenience. She learns that a man can stop.

[BOARD: Ask what you cannot grade.]


## SEGMENT 3 · The Word

WES: Deuteronomy six, verse seven. A house that talks while it walks.

JONATHON: "Impress them on your children. Talk about them when you sit at home and when you walk along the road, when you lie down and when you get up."

WES: Sit. Walk. Lie down. Get up. That is not a family meeting. That is a Saturday ride and a bedtime and a shoulder of a road. If the only time you talk about God and men and grief is a formal talk, she will learn that faith is a presentation. I want her to learn it is a pull-over.

JONATHON: I have done presentations. I have not done enough walking-along-the-road. The road is where she asked for a question I did not know. I keep trying to impress things I have already decided. The verse is impress them, the words of God, not impress her with how prepared I am.

WES: We're about halfway. Wes's book is at walksteadfast.com if you want the longer chapter on a daughter watching. We are not walking a catalog. We are talking about a cereal bowl.

[BOARD: Proverbs 22:6 (NIV) · "Start children off on the way they should go, and even when they are old they will not turn from it."]


## SEGMENT 4 · The work voice

WES: Tell the doorway. I have been waiting on it since you hinted.

JONATHON: Saturday I was on a call with a Corpus Christi contractor, unit down. Ruby stood in the doorway with a cereal bowl to her ear. Oh, man. Oh, I hear you. Absolutely. And the nod. Theo laughed. Dani sipped her coffee. In the truck she said, Dad, why do you talk nicer on the phone. I said that is my work voice. She asked if she could have it. I did the voice at her and let it be a bit. Then I sat with it. I have a coach voice too. I moved it once instead of fixing it. Same voice. Different stranger. She is learning which voice is for family and which voice is for people I need something from.

WES: That should bother you. It bothers me. I have a church voice and a house voice. The house voice is shorter. The church voice is kinder. A daughter notices. She will marry a man with a voice, and she will have a map from you whether you meant to draw one or not.

JONATHON: Bedtime she prays, then says your turn. For two years I said you did good. Amen. Monday night Dani was at work. Ruby said can I go first next time. I said you can go now. I already went. Then I prayed over her by name, out loud. She asks questions. She is brave about worms. I ended, and help her be nice to Theo most of the time. She said that is mine. Then, you can have it. Nobody prayed over me by name after I was nine. My mom prayed behind her door, about money. I can still hear the door.

WES: You gave her the line back. That is a man treating a girl as a person. Not a mascot. Not a project. A person who wrote a heading and will let you borrow it. I want that more than I want a perfect prayer.

[BOARD: Her name belongs in a prayer.]


## SEGMENT 5 · Pushback

WES: Push. The man with a teenager who will not talk. And the man who says his daughter is fine because she makes good grades.

JONATHON: If she will not talk, stop quizzing. Ask one thing you do not know. Then wait longer than is comfortable. A drive-through helps. Eyes forward. I will say more about that Friday. Grades are not a soul. I was a good-grade kid with a shut door and headphones. Fine through the door is not fine.

WES: The man who had no dad thinks he cannot do this. You can still ask a question you do not know. You can still pull over. You can still say a name at bedtime. You do not need a childhood you were not given to give a girl a person. You need a Saturday and a mouth.

JONATHON: Dani dated a couple of disasters in college, her word, even with a dad who came to everything. Showing up is not a guarantee. It is still the work. I do not get to skip the work because the work is not magic. Magic is how men talk themselves out of Thursday.


## SEGMENT 6 · This week's challenge

WES: Ask your daughter one question you do not know the answer to. Do not finish her sentence. If she is grown, still ask. If she is seven, still ask. If you have no daughter, ask the girl you actually have, a niece, a student, a sister. Do not make this a speech for men with perfect photos.

JONATHON: Pray her name out loud once this week. If you have only said you did good, amen, for two years, change it Monday. She may give you a line you can borrow. Take it. Do not improve it.

WES: Last few minutes. Thursday they fish. I will not be there. My brother will. I am trying to be a man who can let another man teach my friend's girl without inserting a lesson. That is also watching. She will see who talks and who hands a worm.

JONATHON: I will ask the man about the worm. I will not answer for him. If I come home with a story about how well I taught her, I lied. I am going to learn. That is the sentence I want her to watch.

WES: I still hear I thought you were mad at me. I was afraid. She read it as anger. I cannot go back to fifteen. I can ask a better question now. I can leave a wound open instead of painting it.

JONATHON: I still hear the door when my mom prayed. I can open a door now. That is the whole assignment. A name, out loud, with the door open. I do not need a better childhood to do it.

WES: I keep wanting this to resolve in one conversation, and it will not. The assignment is the next honest sentence, said on time, without a speech attached. I have left too many good talks on a table because I wanted a better closing line than the one I had.

JONATHON: Honestly, I will try to add the speech anyway. Somebody should stop me. If you are sitting in a driveway doing that too, write the three sentences down before you dial, then read only those three. The fourth sentence is where I get cute and ruin it.

WES: I want this back in your house, not in ours. Pick the one door that is yours. Not your brother's. Not a man on a screen. Yours. Put a day on it before supper, even if the day is tomorrow and the sentence is clumsy.

JONATHON: Mine is already on a calendar. That is the only reason I trust it. A mood is not a plan, and I have used moods for years to sound wise while I stayed still. I do not want to sound wise. I want to be on time.

WES: If you fail the first try, the second try still counts. I have to say that every time or I will use the miss as a way out and then tell a story about the miss like the story was the obedience.

JONATHON: I used the first miss as a way out for most of my twenties. I am trying not to do that at thirty-seven. It is harder than any capacitor, and it is also more ordinary than I want to admit at this table.

## SEGMENT · Sitting with it

WES: I have watched men collect stories like this and never change a Tuesday. Do not be that man this week. Change one Tuesday. Then tell one person you did, because a secret obedience turns back into a mood by Thursday.

JONATHON: I am the man who collects stories. I have a notes app full of them. The only ones that count are the ones that got a day and a time and a person who knows I said I would do it.

WES: I want the listener who thinks this does not apply because his house is quieter. Quiet is not settled. You already know that if you have sat through a polite holiday and called it peace.

JONATHON: My house can be loud and still be a cease-fire. Volume is not the test. The test is whether the true thing got said to the person who had to hear it, not to a mug at this table.

WES: I will check this on Friday, which is the only reason I trust myself. A public calendar is a kind of mercy for a man who likes his recliner and likes to call the recliner wisdom.

JONATHON: I will hold you to it, and I want somebody holding me. That is why I say the time out loud even when it makes me look late. Looking late is better than looking peaceful and being dug in.

WES: A steadfast man does the small thing while it is still small. That is the whole show, said again, because we forget it by Wednesday and start waiting to feel ready, and the ready never comes first.

JONATHON: I forget it by Tuesday afternoon. Ruby does not. She has a better memory for what I said I would do than any review I have ever run, and she does not care that I had a good reason.

WES: If this feels like too many stories, pick one. One house. One name. One time. That is enough weight for seven days, and it is more than most of us will actually carry if we get proud of the list.

WES: I have a habit of saving the true sentence for a better room. There is no better room. The kitchen is the room. The truck is the room. The porch after the dishes is the room. Say it there.

JONATHON: I have rehearsed in better rooms my whole adult life. The rehearsal is how I stay safe. Dani can hear the difference between a rehearsal and a sentence. She stops me when I start handing people a door before they are in the room.

WES: Some of you will try to make this spiritual so you do not have to be specific. Prayer is good. A day and a name is still required. Do not baptize delay and call it discernment.

JONATHON: I have baptized delay. I have called it being careful. Careful is sometimes fear with a pressed shirt. I am trying to tell those two apart before I waste another month.

WES: The man who says he will start after the busy season is lying to himself the same way I did in my forties. There is always another season. Do the small thing inside the busy, or you will never do it.

JONATHON: HVAC sales does not hand you a quiet month. If I wait for one I will still be waiting when Theo has a license. I have to put the true thing on a Tuesday that already has a meeting.

WES: I do not need you to feel ready. I need you to be on time. Ready is a feeling. On time is a clock. I have trusted the feeling too long, and it has made me late to people I love.

JONATHON: I wait for ready like it is a weather window. Then the window closes and I tell a funny story about almost doing it. I do not want another funny story. I want a clumsy sentence that landed.

WES: Look at the next seven days and circle one hour. Not a vibe. An hour. If you cannot find an hour, you are not too busy. You are hiding inside busy, and I have done that with a laminated list and called it love.

JONATHON: I hide inside a full calendar better than anyone I know. A full calendar can still be a man running from one phone call. I am trying to leave a hole in Thursday on purpose.

WES: If your people are used to you disappearing into a screen after supper, tell them you are going to be late to the recliner this week. Then be late to the recliner. That is a kind of going first nobody applauds.

JONATHON: Theo notices the recliner more than he notices my speeches. If I stay at the table ten extra minutes he treats it like a holiday. That is how small this has to be to matter.
